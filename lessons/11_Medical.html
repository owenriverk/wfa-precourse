Overview: Medical vs. Trauma

Medical emergencies are illnesses rather than injuries: stroke, seizures, heart attacks, diabetic crises. Trauma usually announces itself. Medical emergencies can be subtle, and in the wilderness you're the one who has to recognize and manage them.

Key points

AEIOU-TIPS covers most causes of altered mental status.
Not sure of the cause? Treat for low blood sugar (hypoglycemia). Glucose won't significantly hurt a patient whose sugar is high.
Chest pain can be cardiac even in young people. If you suspect the heart, always evacuate.
FAST stroke assessment: Face, Arm, Speech, Time.
Time is brain. Stroke is time-sensitive, and early hospital care prevents brain damage.

Altered Mental Status (AMS)

AEIOU-TIPS mnemonic

A systematic run through the causes of AMS. Work it like a gear check: top to bottom, not just until something fits.

A Alcohol/toxins
E Epilepsy/seizure (postictal state)
I Insulin (hypoglycemia, hyperglycemia)
O Overdose (medications, drugs)
U Uremia (kidney failure)
T Trauma (head injury, hypoxia, shock)
I Infection (sepsis, meningitis, encephalitis)
P Psychosis (psychiatric emergency)
S Syncope/Stroke/Shock

Assessment approach

  • Quick AVPU: What's their level of consciousness?
  • SAMPLE history: Any clues to the cause?
  • Blood glucose: Check it if you have a glucometer. Low blood sugar is reversible and an immediate threat.
  • Vital signs: Fever (infection), fast heart rate (shock, overdose), slow heart rate (head injury)
  • Physical exam: Look for clues: needle marks (overdose), head trauma, focal neurologic deficits

Treatment for any AMS

  • Airway: Position them to protect it.
  • Position: Recovery position if unconscious (and no spine concern)
  • Keep them warm: Prevent hypothermia.
  • Glucose: If you suspect low blood sugar and they can swallow, give glucose.
  • Oxygen: If you have it and suspect hypoxia
  • Evacuation: Any AMS that persists needs evaluation.

Seizures

Types of seizures

  • Generalized tonic-clonic (grand mal): Loss of consciousness, a stiff body (tonic), then jerking (clonic). The most dramatic type.
  • Absence: Brief staring spells, no jerking
  • Focal/partial: Jerking in one limb or one side of the body

Causes

  • Epilepsy (seizure disorder)
  • Head trauma
  • Low blood sugar (hypoglycemia)
  • Low oxygen (hypoxia)
  • Low sodium (hyponatremia)
  • High fever
  • Toxins or drugs

Field management during a seizure

  • Don't restrain them. It can cause injury.
  • Don't put anything in their mouth. It can damage teeth or the airway.
  • Protect the head with padding if any is nearby.
  • Time the seizure for the medical team.
  • Clear the area of hazards.
  • Roll them onto their side if possible, to protect the airway.
  • Watch breathing and color for cyanosis (turning blue).

After the seizure (post-ictal phase)

  • After a seizure: Confusion and drowsiness for minutes to hours. That's normal.
  • They may not remember the seizure happened.
  • Reassure and reorient them, probably more than once.

When to evacuate

  • Always evacuate for:
    • A first-ever seizure
    • A seizure lasting over 5 minutes (status epilepticus, which is life-threatening)
    • Multiple seizures without full recovery in between
    • Focal deficits after the seizure
    • Known epilepsy with an unusual seizure pattern
    • A seizure after head trauma
  • Known epilepsy: If the seizure is typical for them, short, and they fully recover, they may not need evacuation. Still monitor closely.

Diabetic Emergencies

Hypoglycemia (low blood sugar)

Definition: Blood glucose under 70 mg/dL

  • Onset: Rapid, over minutes to hours
  • Signs:
    • Confusion, altered mental status
    • Sweating (diaphoresis), tremor
    • Fast heart rate (tachycardia)
    • Pale, cool skin
    • Hunger
    • Anxiety, personality change
  • Treatment, conscious patient:
    • Glucose tablets, juice, candy, or sugar
    • Reassess in 15 minutes
    • If they've improved, follow with longer-acting carbs (granola, crackers)
  • Treatment, unconscious patient:
    • Nothing by mouth: no food, drink, or glucose gel. It's an aspiration risk, and almost none absorbs through the cheek.
    • Protect the airway: put them in the recovery position.
    • Emergent evacuation. An unconscious diabetic can't be treated in the field.

Hyperglycemia (high blood sugar)

Definition: Blood glucose over 300 mg/dL (can be over 400-500 in DKA)

  • Onset: Gradual, over hours to days
  • Signs:
    • Excessive thirst and urination
    • Fruity-smelling breath (ketones, in DKA)
    • Kussmaul respirations (deep, fast breathing in DKA)
    • Dehydration
    • AMS in severe DKA (diabetic ketoacidosis)
  • Treatment:
    • Hydration is the main treatment
    • Encourage them to drink water
    • Nothing in the field reverses high blood sugar quickly
    • Evacuate for DKA. It's serious.

When in doubt: give sugar

Only if they can swallow. If you can't tell whether the AMS is low or high blood sugar, give glucose, as long as they're awake enough to swallow safely. It won't significantly harm a high-sugar patient and can save a low-sugar one.

Chest Pain & Cardiac Emergencies

Cardiac or non-cardiac?

Cardiac causes: ACS (acute coronary syndrome, meaning angina or MI), pericarditis

Non-cardiac: Musculoskeletal, esophageal, pleurisy, pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax, HAPE

  • Signs that point to the heart:
    • Crushing or pressure-type pain
    • Pain that spreads to the arm, jaw, or back
    • Sweating (diaphoresis), nausea
    • History of heart disease, or risk factors for it
    • Age over 45
  • Any concern it's the heart: Treat it as ACS until proven otherwise.

Field management of suspected cardiac chest pain

  • Position: Usually sitting up, in whatever position is most comfortable
  • Aspirin: 325 mg regular, or 4 × 81 mg chewable. Have them chew it so it works faster. Only if they're not allergic and have no active bleeding or ulcer.
  • Nitroglycerin: Only if it's the patient's own prescription. Do not use someone else's.
  • Oxygen: If you have it and their SpO2 is low
  • Urgent evacuation for all suspected cardiac chest pain

Stroke: Time is Brain

FAST stroke assessment

F Face droop: Ask them to smile. Does one side droop?
A Arm weakness: Have them raise both arms. Does one drift down?
S Speech: Have them repeat a simple phrase. Slurred or strange?
T Time: Call for help now. Thrombolytics work within 3-4.5 hours.
BE-FAST. Add Balance (sudden loss of balance or coordination) and Eyes (sudden vision loss or change) to FAST. Strokes in the back of the brain (posterior circulation) can slip past FAST alone.

Other stroke signs

  • Sudden severe headache (the worst of their life)
  • Vision changes
  • Sudden confusion or trouble understanding
  • Sudden weakness or numbness on one side

Why time is brain

  • Clot-busting drugs (thrombolytics): Effective within 3-4.5 hours of symptom onset. Given early, they can prevent permanent brain damage.
  • Outside this window: Options are limited, and brain damage is already happening.
  • In the field: Recognize the signs and start an urgent evacuation. Every minute counts.

Field management

  • Watch the airway: Stroke affects swallowing, so aspiration is a risk.
  • Head position: Slightly raised (15-30°) if blood pressure isn't low
  • Nothing by mouth: Same aspiration risk
  • Nothing to reverse out here: You can't undo a stroke in the field.
  • Urgent evacuation: Time to the hospital is the treatment.

Stroke mimics

  • Hypoglycemia: Can look like a stroke. Check blood glucose first and treat if it's low.
  • Todd's paralysis: Weakness after a seizure. It's temporary and resolves.
  • Complex migraine: Can cause focal neurologic symptoms

Case Scenarios

Scenario 1: Sudden weakness on a backpacking trip

Setting: On day 3 of a 7-day trip, a 55-year-old develops sudden weakness in one arm and mild trouble speaking. The group is 8 hours from the trailhead, with no cell service but a satellite communicator.

Recognition: Stroke. Sudden onset, and FAST is positive for arm weakness and speech.

Field actions:

  • Run the full FAST assessment.
  • Check blood glucose to rule out low blood sugar as the cause.
  • Activate the satellite communicator immediately and note the time symptoms started. The thrombolytic window runs from it.
  • Position: head raised if blood pressure isn't low
  • Nothing by mouth
  • Monitor airway and breathing.

Evacuation: Urgent. Helicopter or the fastest evacuation possible. The goal is a hospital within 3-4.5 hours of symptom onset for thrombolytic therapy.

Scenario 2: Diabetic confusion at camp

Setting: A diabetic patient on a guided trip gets confused and irritable after a 10-mile hike. The group has a glucometer and first aid kit. The patient says they skipped lunch, too busy hiking.

Recognition: Likely low blood sugar: a skipped meal, confusion, irritability.

Immediate actions:

  • Check blood glucose with the glucometer.
  • If it's under 70 mg/dL, give glucose tablets or juice immediately.
  • Reassess in 15 minutes.
  • If they've improved, follow with more substantial carbohydrates and protein.

Evacuation: If glucose clears the confusion and they fully recover, they may not need evacuation. Rest, eat, monitor, and make sure they eat before moving on. Ten miles on no lunch is how this started.

Lesson Summary

The short version

AEIOU-TIPS covers most causes of altered mental status.
Low blood sugar is reversible. Always consider it, and treat if you suspect it.
Seizures: protect, don't restrain, time the event.
Diabetic emergencies: low blood sugar (rapid, emergency treatment) vs. high blood sugar (slow, comfort care).
Chest pain: if the heart's a concern, treat as ACS. Aspirin plus urgent evacuation.
Stroke: FAST. Time to the hospital is the treatment (thrombolytics within 3-4.5 hours).
When the cause is unclear, treat the most immediately life-threatening condition.

Glossary

Altered Mental Status (AMS)
A change from the patient's baseline in consciousness, confusion, or behavior
AEIOU-TIPS
A mnemonic for the causes of altered mental status
Seizure
Uncontrolled electrical activity in the brain causing loss of consciousness, jerking, or both
Status Epilepticus
A seizure over 5 minutes, or repeated seizures without recovery. Life-threatening.
Post-Ictal Phase
The confused, drowsy period after a seizure. Normal recovery.
Hypoglycemia
Low blood sugar (under 70 mg/dL). An emergency, rapidly reversible with glucose.
Hyperglycemia
High blood sugar (over 300 mg/dL). A chronic problem with no quick field fix.
Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS)
Heart attack or unstable angina. A cardiac emergency needing immediate intervention.
FAST Stroke Assessment
Face, Arm, Speech, Time: a quick check for stroke symptoms
Stroke
Sudden loss of brain function from a blocked (ischemic) or bleeding (hemorrhagic) blood vessel
Todd's Paralysis
Temporary weakness after a seizure that resolves as post-ictal confusion clears
