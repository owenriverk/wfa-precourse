First Aid Kits & Trip Preparation

Everything you've learned in this course runs on the gear you brought and the planning you did before you left. Build a kit that matches the trip — and know every item in it.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you'll be able to:

  • Explain why training and planning matter more than any single piece of gear
  • Build a core wilderness first aid kit, and justify each item by the skill it supports
  • Pick the right over-the-counter medications, and state the scope rules for helping someone take them
  • Scale a kit by time-to-help, trip length, group size, and activity
  • Get a group ready before a trip: medical information, a communication plan, and kit upkeep

Every skill in this course has a physical counterpart. Bleeding control needs gauze and a tourniquet. Splinting needs something rigid and something soft. Calling for rescue needs a device that works where you're going. This lesson turns the course into a packing list.

The kit is not the response. Your assessment system, your decisions, and your hands are the response. The kit just keeps them supplied. A full hose bed is only as good as the crew that's practiced pulling it. A well-trained person with a minimal kit outperforms a well-stocked kit with an untrained owner.

This lesson pairs with the course's interactive kit checklist. Tick it off as you build your own kit, and print it for restocking.

Key points

Gear only helps if you've practiced the skill it supports
Scale the kit by time-to-help, not just days on the trail
Preparation is part of the kit: group medical info, a communication plan, restocking

Kit Principles

Skills over stuff

Before any item earns a place in your pack, ask one question: "Have I practiced using this?" A tourniquet you've never applied, a splint you've never molded, or a wound-closure strip you've never placed is dead weight. Worse, it's false confidence. Buy less, practice more.

Scale by time-to-help

Kit size depends less on how many days you'll be out than on how long help would take to reach you. An afternoon in a remote canyon can justify more kit than a week of car camping near a town.

TripAdjust the baseline
Short day hike, front-countryTrim to gloves, bleeding control, blister care, basic meds, whistle, phone
Full day, remoteThe full core kit, plus a satellite messenger if you'll be out of coverage
Multi-day backcountryDouble the consumables (gauze, gloves, meds), and add a low-reading thermometer and repair items
Group or trip leaderScale consumables by head count, collect medical information from every member before you leave, and carry group shelter

Protect the kit itself

  • Waterproof everything. Wet gauze is no gauze. Use dry bags or zip-top bags. The bags double as irrigation reservoirs and improvised ice packs.
  • Organize for the emergency, not the shelf. Bleeding-control items go on top, where shaking hands can grab them. Group items by problem, not by size.
  • Know your kit blind. You might be opening it in the dark, in the rain, with cold fingers. Repack it the same way every time.
  • Restock after every trip. The gauze you used last time is the gauze you won't have next time. Check medication expiration dates seasonally.
Pre-built kits
A commercial kit is a fine skeleton to start from. Most ship with flimsy gloves, no tourniquet, and no irrigation syringe. Upgrade the gloves, add what matches your training, and pull out what you can't use. Then it's your kit.

Key points

Every item must be backed by a practiced skill
Time-to-help drives kit size more than trip length does
Waterproof, organized for emergencies, restocked after every trip

The Core Kit

This is a solid day-trip baseline for two people, sorted by the problem each group of items solves. Each group maps to the lesson that teaches its use. If a line surprises you, that's the lesson to review.

Protect yourself (Lesson 1)

  • Nitrile gloves, 3–4 pairs: not latex (allergy risk). They tear, so carry spares.
  • CPR barrier mask: a pocket mask or a keychain shield
  • Hand sanitizer: before and after patient contact

Stop bleeding (Lesson 4)

  • Gauze pads (4×4"), 6+: direct pressure is the workhorse
  • Roller gauze, 2 rolls: holds pressure dressings in place
  • Commercial tourniquet (CAT-style): for limb bleeding that pressure can't control. Practice with it before you need it.
  • Hemostatic gauze (optional): for junctional wounds a tourniquet can't reach. Pack it and hold pressure.

Wound care (Lesson 4)

  • Irrigation syringe (10–20 mL): pressure irrigation with clean water is the best infection prevention you can carry
  • Adhesive bandages, wound closure strips, non-adherent pads
  • Antibiotic ointment or petroleum jelly: single-use packets stay clean
  • Medical tape: cloth tape holds when skin is wet
  • Blister kit: moleskin or kinesiology-style tape. It's the most-used item in any hiking kit.

Bones & joints (Lesson 6)

  • Moldable splint (SAM-style): one splint, dozens of configurations
  • Elastic wrap (3–4"): sprains, securing splints, pressure dressings
  • Triangular bandages, 2: slings, swathes, and ties

Tools & documentation

  • Trauma shears: cut clothing off, not around (Lesson 2: expose to assess)
  • Fine-point tweezers: splinters and tick removal (Lesson 12)
  • SOAP note forms and a pencil: print the course field reference card. A pencil writes wet and cold (Lesson 1).
  • Headlamp, emergency blanket or bivy, duct tape: light, shock care and a hypothermia wrap (Lesson 9), and repairs

Call for help (Lesson 13)

  • Whistle: three blasts signal distress. It carries farther than your voice, and it never goes hoarse.
  • Phone with backup battery: airplane mode preserves the charge
  • Satellite messenger or PLB: when you're truly remote, the highest-value item in the kit
  • Paper map: you can't give rescuers a location you don't know
Improvising stretches a kit. It doesn't replace one. Trekking poles become splints, bandanas become slings, and sleeping pads become litters. Bleeding-control supplies, gloves, and medications are the exception: there's no good field improvisation for them. Those always ride in the kit.

Key points

Organize the kit by problem: protection, bleeding, wounds, bones, tools, communication
Bleeding control, gloves, and meds can't be improvised. Never trim those.
Use the interactive kit checklist to build and restock your own

Scope rules for a wilderness first aider

  • You may offer over-the-counter medications at package doses to an alert adult who consents. Show them the label.
  • You may assist a patient with their own prescribed medications: their epinephrine auto-injector (Lesson 10), their inhaler, their nitroglycerin.
  • You do not carry or administer prescription medications that aren't yours or the patient's.
  • Never give anything by mouth to a patient who isn't fully alert or can't swallow safely (Lesson 5).
Label everything
Loose white pills in a zip-top bag are a guessing game, and they're unusable in an emergency. You can't offer a medication you can't positively identify, along with its dose and expiration. Repackage into labeled containers, or carry the manufacturer's blister packs.

Pre-trip preparation

  • Group medical information: before the trip, collect each member's allergies, medications, and relevant history. It's SAMPLE, gathered in advance (Lesson 2). Know who carries an epinephrine auto-injector or an inhaler, and where they keep it.
  • Communication plan: know your coverage, program local emergency contacts, and leave a trip plan with someone at home: route, party size, return time, and when to call for help if you're overdue (Lesson 13).
  • Kit check: restock consumables, replace expired medications, and confirm every group member knows where the kit rides in the pack.

Key points

OTC meds at package doses, with consent, to alert patients only
Assist with the patient's own prescriptions. Never carry or give anyone else's.
A trip plan left at home is the cheapest rescue device that exists

Scenarios

Scenario 1: The Borrowed Kit

Setting: At the trailhead for a remote day hike, a friend hands you a large, brand-new commercial first aid kit. "We're covered. This thing has everything." You flip through it. There's one pair of vinyl gloves, thirty adhesive bandages, a foil blanket, ointment packets, and a triangular bandage. There's no irrigation syringe, no tourniquet, one small roll of gauze, and no medications beyond aspirin.

Questions to consider:

  • What can this kit actually treat well, and what can't it?
  • Which gaps matter most for a remote day hike?
  • What would you add from your own supplies before leaving the trailhead?

What's going on: This kit handles small cuts and blisters, the most common problems. It doesn't handle the most dangerous ones. It can't stop serious bleeding (one gauze roll, no tourniquet), can't properly clean a backcountry wound (no irrigation), and offers little for allergic reactions or pain.

What to add first: more gauze and a tourniquet; an irrigation syringe (or, as a field improvisation, a spare zip-top bag with a pinhole); several pairs of nitrile gloves; and OTC medications, including diphenhydramine and ibuprofen. Find out who in the group carries prescription epinephrine or an inhaler, and where it is.

Judge a kit by its worst day. That's the worst problem it has to handle at that distance from help, not how many pockets it has.

Scenario 2: The Group Trip Inventory

Setting: You're leading five friends on a three-day rafting trip on a remote river. There's no road access between the put-in and the take-out, and no cell coverage in the canyon. It's the week before, and you're putting the group kit together.

Questions to consider:

  • How does this trip change the kit from your solo day-hike baseline?
  • What preparation matters beyond the physical kit?
  • What activity-specific risks should shape your choices?

What's going on: Six people, three days, no exit, no coverage. Every scaling factor points up. Consumables scale by head count. The time-to-help makes a satellite messenger close to mandatory (Lesson 13). The river adds drowning and cold-water immersion risk (Lesson 14), and it guarantees everything gets wet.

Kit changes: double-bagged dry storage; a full hypothermia setup (insulation layer, blanket or bivy, and sugar-rich food, per Lesson 9); double the gauze, gloves, and medications; a low-reading thermometer; blister and sun care for six.

Preparation: collect SAMPLE-style medical information from all five friends before the trip. Confirm who carries prescription epinephrine or inhalers, and brief the group on where they ride. File a trip plan with a contact at home, including the overdue-call time. If the messenger supports check-ins, agree on a communication schedule.

Most of this job happens on dry land. On a group trip, the leader's most important first aid work happens before anyone gets in the boat.

Lesson Summary

Your first aid kit is the physical half of this course. Build it around the skills you've practiced, organize it around the problems it solves, and size it by how long help would take to reach you. Preparation counts as part of the kit too: group medical information, a trip plan left at home, and restocking after every trip.

Glossary

Time-to-Help
How long professional care would take to reach you, or you to reach it. It's the main driver of kit size and contents.
Consumables
Kit items that get used up in a single response: gauze, gloves, medications, tape. They scale with group size and have to be restocked after every use.
Hemostatic Gauze
Gauze treated with a clotting agent, packed into wounds where a tourniquet can't work. It needs firm, sustained pressure.
Trip Plan
Your route, party size, and return time, written down and left with a contact at home, plus an agreed time to call for help if the party is overdue.
PLB / Satellite Messenger
Devices that summon help beyond cell coverage. Lesson 13 covers how each one works.

Key points

Skills first: every item must map to something you can actually do
Scale by time-to-help. Double consumables for groups and multi-day trips.
OTC meds with consent, to alert patients. Assist with the patient's own prescriptions.
Waterproof it, organize it by problem, restock it after every trip
