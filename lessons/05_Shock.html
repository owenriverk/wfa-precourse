Shock Recognition & Treatment

Overview

Learning Objectives

  • Explain what shock is, and what goes wrong in the body when tissues don't get enough blood flow
  • Name the four types of shock: hypovolemic, distributive, obstructive, and cardiogenic
  • Spot the early and late signs of shock in a patient in the backcountry
  • Treat shock with what you have in the field
  • Decide how urgently a shock patient needs to get out

Shock is the body failing to get enough oxygen to its organs and tissues. It's common after trauma, and it gets missed a lot. Early shock looks like a pale, anxious person having a bad day, which on a hard trip describes half the group. Miss it, and treatment starts late. Late treatment is how shock kills people.

Shock kills quietly, and the early signs are easy to wave off. Learn to spot them before the blood pressure falls. By then you're behind.

Left alone, it only gets worse. A patient in compensated shock can look fairly well, then slide fast into decompensated shock and death if nobody treats them. Catch it early and treat it early.

What is Shock?

Definition and pathophysiology

Shock is inadequate tissue perfusion. Not enough blood reaches the tissues, cells run short on oxygen, and they can't keep up with their own metabolic demands.

Oxygen delivery comes down to one relationship, the oxygen delivery relationship:

Oxygen Delivery (DO₂) = Cardiac Output (CO) × Arterial Oxygen Content (CaO₂)

In plain terms, getting oxygen to the tissues takes two things:
  • Enough cardiac output (heart rate × stroke volume)
  • Enough oxygen in the blood (hemoglobin and oxygen saturation)

Why cells need oxygen

Cells use oxygen to make ATP, the energy that runs everything they do. Cut the oxygen and they switch to anaerobic metabolism. That produces lactate, and it sets off a chain:

  • Lactic acidosis
  • Less ATP
  • Cells stop working, then die
  • Organ systems fail

How the body compensates

When perfusion first drops, the body pushes back:

Faster heart rate (tachycardia). The sympathetic nervous system speeds up the heart to hold cardiac output steady while each beat moves less blood, as it does after blood loss.

Vasoconstriction. Peripheral vessels clamp down to hold central perfusion pressure and send blood to the brain and heart.

Faster breathing (tachypnea). Breathing speeds up to pull in as much oxygen as it can.

Compensated vs. decompensated shock

Compensated shock: The compensation is working. Blood pressure is normal, but you can see the sympathetic system straining: anxiety, a fast pulse, fast breathing. Treat it here and the patient usually survives.

Decompensated shock: The compensation has failed. Blood pressure drops, mental status changes, and organs start to fail. It's life-threatening, so manage it as the emergency it is.

Picture an engine pumping a hose line off a tank that's slowly draining. The operator keeps throttling up, and the pressure gauge holds steady. It holds right up until the tank's nearly dry, and then it falls fast. Blood pressure is the gauge. Heart rate is the throttle.

Watch the pulse, not the cuff. Low blood pressure is a late sign of shock. A normal reading doesn't rule shock out. Look for a fast heart rate and the other early signs.

Types of Shock

Shock is sorted by what's causing it. Knowing the type tells you what to go after.

Type Cause Example Scenario Key Field Signs Field Treatment Priority
Hypovolemic Lost blood or body fluids Bleeding, severe dehydration, burns Fast pulse, pale skin, weak pulse, low BP (late) Stop the bleeding, position, keep warm, evacuate
Distributive Vessels dilate hard and lose their tone Sepsis, anaphylaxis, neurogenic (spinal injury) Fast pulse, flushed or blotchy skin, wide pulse pressure (early) Treat the cause (help the patient use their own epinephrine auto-injector for anaphylaxis; spinal motion restriction for neurogenic), keep warm, evacuate
Obstructive Something physically blocks blood flow Tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, massive PE Fast pulse, bulging neck veins (JVD), muffled heart sounds, trouble breathing Recognize the pattern, position of comfort, emergent evacuation. Definitive treatment happens in a hospital.
Cardiogenic The heart fails as a pump Heart attack (MI), severe arrhythmia Fast pulse, fluid in the lungs (pulmonary edema), pale, weak pulse Sit them upright if conscious, oxygen if you have it, urgent evacuation

Hypovolemic shock (the most common in the wilderness)

The body has lost volume: blood from a bleed, or other fluids from dehydration or severe burns. Treatment is to stop the bleeding, give oral fluids when it's safe, keep them flat on their back (supine) and warm, and get them out.

Distributive shock

Septic shock: A severe infection makes the vessels dilate and leak. In the wilderness it usually builds over hours, for example from an infected wound. Keep an eye on the wound everyone agreed looked fine on day two.

Anaphylactic shock: An allergic reaction releases histamine fast, and the vessels dilate hard. It's a true emergency, and it needs epinephrine immediately.

Neurogenic shock: A spinal cord injury cuts the sympathetic signals that keep the vessels toned. The vessels go slack and the heart slows. That pairing is unusual, and it's the tell: low blood pressure with a slow heart rate. It can last days to weeks. Field care: keep them flat, maintain spinal motion restriction, work hard to prevent heat loss, and evacuate.

Obstructive shock

Something physically blocks blood getting back to the heart or out of it. Examples: tension pneumothorax (a collapsed lung compressing the heart), cardiac tamponade (fluid around the heart), and a massive pulmonary embolism. Field care is to recognize the pattern, put them in a position of comfort, and make it an emergent evacuation. Definitive treatment happens in a hospital.

Cardiogenic shock

The heart itself fails as a pump, from a heart attack (MI) or a severe arrhythmia. It's less common in the wilderness, but you still have to recognize it. Treatment is supportive positioning and an urgent evacuation.

Recognizing Shock

Early (compensated) signs

These are subtle, and they're the ones that matter:

  • Fast heart rate (tachycardia): over 100 a minute at rest. It's the earliest sign you can measure.
  • Fast breathing (tachypnea): over 20 breaths a minute
  • Anxiety and restlessness: they feel ill and scared
  • Pale or cool skin: from vasoconstriction. Check the core, not just the hands.
  • Weak pulse: less force under your fingers

Late (decompensated) signs

These mean compensation is failing and the slide toward irreversible shock has started:

  • Slow heart rate (bradycardia): the heart itself is failing. This is a bad sign.
  • Low blood pressure (hypotension): systolic under 90 mmHg
  • Altered mental status: confusion, lethargy, loss of consciousness
  • Mottled skin: blotchy and bluish (cyanotic)
  • Cold extremities: severe peripheral vasoconstriction
  • Weak or absent pulse: even the central pulse is hard to feel

Vital sign thresholds

Finding Concerning Value Interpretation
Systolic BP <90 mmHg Decompensated shock (treat immediately)
Heart Rate >100 bpm at rest Early shock, compensating
Respiratory Rate >20 breaths/min Compensating for metabolic acidosis
Mental Status Altered, confused Not enough blood reaching the brain (urgent)

Field Treatment of Shock

Position: flat on the back (supine)

Lay the patient flat on their back. The standard for shock is: flat, prevent heat loss, treat the cause, evacuate. Raising the legs isn't a routine step. If there's no sign of trauma (fainting, dehydration, sepsis), you can raise the feet 6 to 12 inches (15 to 30 cm) while you wait for help, as long as it doesn't cause pain. With any trauma, keep them flat.

  • Flat keeps the heart and brain level, which helps blood reach the brain
  • Don't move a patient with a suspected spine, pelvis, or leg injury just to reposition them
  • Flat makes it easier to watch them and reassess
Exceptions. Someone who's struggling to breathe may need the head and torso raised a little. A conscious patient who's vomiting, or about to, may need the recovery position on their side. Airway and comfort come first.

Keep them warm

Stop the heat loss:

  • Get wet clothing off
  • Cover them with blankets or insulation
  • Don't let them get cold. Hypothermia makes shock worse.
Why warmth matters. Hypothermia causes vasoconstriction and shunts blood away from the skin, which makes shock worse. Shock and hypothermia together push the death rate sharply higher.

Treat the cause

Go after whatever's driving it:

  • Bleeding: direct pressure, and a tourniquet when it's needed
  • Dehydration: oral fluids in small, frequent sips, if they're conscious and can swallow safely
  • Anaphylaxis: help them use their own epinephrine auto-injector, right away
  • Spinal injury: maintain spinal motion restriction (neurogenic shock)

Oral fluids (when it's safe)

If the patient is conscious and alert, can swallow safely, and shows no sign of abdominal injury, you can give them something to drink:

  • Water or a dilute electrolyte drink
  • Small, frequent sips, not big gulps
  • Do not give fluids if they're vomiting, their mental status is altered, or you suspect an abdominal injury. Fluid can make internal bleeding worse.

Monitoring

Recheck vital signs and mental status often:

  • Heart rate and rhythm
  • Blood pressure, if you've got a cuff
  • Skin temperature and color
  • Level of consciousness
  • The trend: getting better or getting worse?

Evacuation urgency

Any patient with signs of shock is an urgent or emergent evacuation.

  • Decompensated shock (low blood pressure, altered mental status): emergent. Evacuate immediately, with ALS (advanced life support) coming to meet you.
  • Compensated shock (fast pulse, anxiety, normal blood pressure): urgent. Evacuate immediately, though they can tolerate a slower move if that's what you've got.
  • Suspected shock from any cause: don't hold the evacuation for field treatment. Nobody at the hospital is grading your splint.

Clinical Scenarios

Scenario 1: Hemorrhagic Shock
A 35-year-old hiker is cutting firewood when the axe slips and opens a deep cut on the inside of the thigh. You get there 10 minutes later. The patient is sitting up and looks anxious. Vitals: HR 112, RR 22, BP 118/82 (baseline unknown), pale skin, and the wound is still bleeding.
What's going on: Compensated hypovolemic shock from blood loss (hemorrhagic shock). The early signs are all here: fast pulse, fast breathing, anxiety, pale skin. The blood pressure is still normal, but this patient is heading for decompensation.

What you do now:
  • A deep thigh wound that's still bleeding, in a patient showing shock, is life-threatening hemorrhage. Put a tourniquet on above the wound now, with direct pressure while it's being readied.
  • Lay them flat. This is trauma, so don't raise the legs.
  • Keep them warm with blankets.
  • Nothing by mouth.
  • Urgent evacuation. Call for a helicopter or organize a ground evacuation right away.
  • Recheck vitals every 5 to 10 minutes and watch for the slide into decompensated shock.
Where it goes: Control the bleeding fast and get the evacuation moving, and this patient has a good chance. Every delay raises the risk of decompensation.
Scenario 2: Anaphylactic Shock
At camp, a 28-year-old woman sits down right on a yellow jacket nest she didn't see. She's stung multiple times and runs for the tent. Within 5 minutes she says it's hard to breathe, her tongue is swelling, and she's itching all over. Vitals: HR 128, RR 28, BP 92/60. Her skin is mottled and flushed, you can hear her wheezing, and her face is swollen.
What's going on: Anaphylactic shock, a type of distributive shock, and a medical emergency. She has a severe allergic reaction plus shock: low blood pressure, a fast heart rate, and poor perfusion.

What you do now:
  • Help her use her own epinephrine auto-injector immediately (0.3 mg into the muscle of the outer thigh). This is what saves her life. Don't delay it.
  • Repeat the epinephrine in 5 to 15 minutes if she isn't improving, or if the symptoms come back.
  • Lay her flat. If breathing is hard, let her sit up a little. If she's breathing fine lying down, raising her feet is an option. There's no trauma here.
  • Manage the airway. The swelling is already compromising it, so watch it closely.
  • Give supplemental oxygen if you have it.
  • After the epinephrine, she can take an oral antihistamine as an add-on. It is never a substitute for epinephrine.
  • Keep her warm.
  • Emergent evacuation to a hospital. Anaphylaxis can come back after it seems to settle (a biphasic reaction).
  • Keep watching her airway and vitals the whole time.
Epinephrine first. It's the only definitive field treatment for anaphylaxis. Don't hold it for any other medication.

Summary & Key Takeaways

The short version:
  • Shock is inadequate tissue perfusion. The cells aren't getting enough oxygen.
  • A fast heart rate is often the earliest sign. Low blood pressure is a late one.
  • Four types: hypovolemic (the most common), distributive, obstructive, cardiogenic.
  • Compensated shock can look fine and still go downhill fast.
  • Field treatment: lay them flat, prevent heat loss, treat the cause, keep checking, and evacuate urgently.
  • Any suspected shock is an urgent evacuation.

What you'll actually see

In the wilderness, you'll mostly see hypovolemic shock from trauma and distributive shock from anaphylaxis or infection. A patient can be in significant shock with a normal blood pressure, so read the whole picture: fast pulse, anxiety, pale skin, weak pulse. Watch the throttle, not the gauge.

Glossary of Terms

Aerobic Metabolism
How cells make energy using oxygen. It produces ATP efficiently and is the normal state. When oxygen runs short, cells switch to anaerobic metabolism.
Anaerobic Metabolism
How cells make energy without oxygen. It produces lactate and less ATP. It happens in shock and hypoxia and leads to lactic acidosis.
Cardiogenic Shock
Shock from the heart failing as a pump (for example, MI or a severe arrhythmia). Less common in the wilderness.
Compensated Shock
Early shock, where the body's compensation (tachycardia, vasoconstriction) holds blood pressure near normal. The patient looks anxious but may not look critically ill.
Decompensated Shock
Late shock, where compensation has failed: blood pressure drops, mental status changes, and organs start to fail. Life-threatening.
Distributive Shock
Shock from vasodilation and lost vascular tone (septic, anaphylactic, neurogenic). Marked by a wide pulse pressure.
Hypovolemic Shock
Shock from lost circulating volume (blood or other fluids). The most common type in trauma and wilderness emergencies.
Lactic Acidosis
Lactate building up in the blood from anaerobic metabolism during shock. It makes cell dysfunction worse and is a marker of tissue hypoxia.
Mottled Skin
Blotchy, bluish (cyanotic) skin in decompensated shock, from severe peripheral vasoconstriction and poor perfusion.
Neurogenic Shock
Shock from a spinal cord injury that knocks out sympathetic tone, so the peripheral vessels dilate. It can last days to weeks. The tell is low blood pressure with a slow heart rate.
Obstructive Shock
Shock from a physical blockage to blood flow (tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, PE). Needs an emergent evacuation; definitive treatment happens in a hospital.
Oxygen Delivery Relationship
Oxygen delivery (DO₂) = cardiac output × arterial oxygen content. Perfusion needs both a working heart and oxygen in the blood.
Perfusion
Blood (and the oxygen it carries) flowing to the tissues. Inadequate perfusion is the core problem in shock.
Shock
Inadequate tissue perfusion and cellular hypoxia. Untreated, it leads to organ failure and death.
Tachycardia
A fast heart rate (over 100 bpm at rest). One of the earliest signs of shock, as the heart works to hold cardiac output.
Tachypnea
Fast breathing (over 20 breaths/min). In shock it comes from sympathetic activation and metabolic acidosis.
Vasoconstriction
Blood vessels narrowing to raise peripheral resistance and send blood to the vital organs. It's one of the main ways the body compensates in shock.
