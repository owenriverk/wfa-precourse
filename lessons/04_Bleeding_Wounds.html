Overview: Bleeding, Wounds & Burns

Bleeding is the most preventable cause of death in trauma, so stopping it comes first. Wound care comes next, to prevent infection and help healing. Then burns. On our trips, exhaust pipes, campfire and stove mishaps, and sun exposure make burns one of the most common soft-tissue injuries you'll actually treat.

X comes before A. Exsanguinating hemorrhage (bleeding out) is the X in XABCDE, and you treat it before the airway. Control massive bleeding immediately.

Why bleeding comes first

  • Massive arterial bleeding can kill in under five minutes, faster than most airway problems develop
  • In many trauma cases, shock from bleeding kills faster than airway obstruction does
  • Hemorrhage control is the skill that saves the most lives in wilderness trauma

Key points

Direct pressure and time control most bleeding.
Obviously life-threatening bleeding from an arm or leg gets a tourniquet immediately. Don't wait for pressure to fail.
Hemostatic agents (QuikClot, Combat Gauze) help a wound clot.
Irrigation is the most important step in wound care.
Don't close bite wounds or punctures. The infection risk is too high.
Cool burns with cool, not ice-cold, running water for 10-20 minutes. Never ice.
A burn with any airway or inhalation involvement (singed nasal hair, hoarseness, sooty sputum) is an emergent evacuation.

Hemorrhage Control

Types of bleeding

  • Arterial (bright red, pulsatile): oxygen-rich blood from the arteries. It's bright red and spurts with each heartbeat. Life-threatening, and it needs control now.
  • Venous (dark red, steady): oxygen-poor blood from the veins. Darker red, with a steady flow. Still serious, but usually easier to control than arterial.
  • Capillary (oozing): a slow ooze from small vessels in the skin. It usually stops on its own with direct pressure.

Direct pressure: the gold standard

  • Technique: firm, continuous pressure directly on the wound, with gloved hands and clean cloth or gauze
  • Duration: at least 10 minutes of continuous pressure, no peeking. Don't lift to check whether it's stopped.
  • If bleeding continues: add more layers on top. Don't pull off the first layer, because that disrupts clotting. Keep the pressure on.
  • Re-bleeding: if it starts bleeding again after you release pressure, reapply direct pressure for another 10 minutes
  • Escalation sequence: direct pressure → wound packing → tourniquet. Raising the limb isn't a bleeding-control step.
No peeking. Everyone wants to check. Lifting the dressing disrupts the clot while it forms, so keep the pressure on for the full 10 minutes.

Pressure dressing

  • Once the bleeding's controlled, wrap an elastic bandage (an ACE wrap or similar) over the gauze pads
  • It holds constant pressure to keep the clot intact
  • It allows continued activity with some protection
  • Watch for signs it's too tight: cold fingers or toes, numbness, tingling, color changes

Tourniquet application

When to use: For obviously life-threatening bleeding from an arm or leg (bright red or pulsatile, blood pooling fast, the patient getting shocky), put a tourniquet on immediately. Don't wait for direct pressure to fail. Direct pressure is for bleeding that isn't clearly life-threatening, or to buy time while the tourniquet's being readied.

  • Indication: obviously life-threatening bleeding from an arm or leg (tourniquet first), or limb bleeding that direct pressure doesn't control
  • Location: 2-3 inches above the wound, not on a joint
  • Tightness: tighten until the bleeding stops completely and the pulse below the tourniquet (distal pulse) is gone
  • If bleeding continues: put a second tourniquet on just above or beside the first
  • Document time: write the time it went on directly on the patient's skin (the forehead works) or on the tourniquet
  • Don't remove it in the field: once it's on, leave it. Loosening it risks massive re-bleeding.
  • Types: commercial tourniquets (CAT, SOFTT-W) work better than improvised ones

Improvised tourniquet

  • Use material at least 2 inches wide, like a belt or rolled cloth
  • Windlass technique: wrap it around the limb above the wound, put a stick through the loop, and twist to tighten
  • Less effective than a commercial one, but it can work in an emergency
  • Write down the time clearly
Don't hesitate. A tourniquet is safe in place for at least 2 hours. Fear of tissue damage should never delay one. A patient can bleed to death while you wait for pressure to work. When it's indicated, use it.

Wound packing: junctional hemorrhage

When to use: wounds where a tourniquet can't go, like the groin (inguinal crease) or armpit (axilla)

  • Technique: pack clean gauze tightly into the wound
  • Maintain pressure: don't stuff the gauze in and walk away. Keep constant pressure on it.
  • Hemostatic gauze preferred: gauze impregnated with kaolin or chitosan helps the blood clot
  • Duration: at least 3 minutes of sustained pressure with hemostatic gauze. Plain gauze needs about 10 minutes of sustained pressure.
  • Dressing: once the bleeding's controlled, leave the packing in and put a pressure dressing over it
  • Never pack: chest or abdominal wounds. Packing doesn't reach the bleeding, and it delays care. Neck wounds get sustained direct pressure only, never packing.

Hemostatic agents

  • What they do: gauze impregnated with clotting agents (kaolin, chitosan) boosts the body's own clotting
  • Products: QuikClot, Combat Gauze. Both are common in wilderness first aid kits.
  • Application: pack it tightly into the wound and hold sustained pressure
  • Dwell time: hold pressure for at least 3 minutes
  • Removal: it can be rinsed out, but it's often left in place for transport

Epistaxis (nosebleed)

  • Position: lean forward so blood doesn't drain down the throat. Tilting the head back, the old advice, sends it straight there.
  • Pressure: pinch the soft part of the nose, below the bone, firmly
  • Duration: 10-15 minutes of continuous pressure
  • Cold compress: ice or a cold compress on the bridge of the nose and the face
  • After control: no nose-blowing and no strenuous activity for hours
  • When to evacuate: if it's still bleeding after more than 20 minutes of pressure, or it keeps coming back heavily, evacuate for a medical evaluation

Wound Care & Infection Prevention

Wound assessment

  • Mechanism: how did it happen? A clean cut or a crush? How contaminated is it?
  • Depth: superficial (epidermis), partial thickness (dermis), full thickness (into the subcutaneous tissue)
  • Contamination: clean (surgical), clean-contaminated, contaminated (soil, feces), dirty or infected
  • Neurovascular status: is the blood supply intact past the wound (distal)? Is sensation normal?
  • Time elapsed: how long since the injury? It affects infection risk, which goes up after 8 hours. Note the time and report it to the receiving clinician.

Wound types and management

Abrasion (road rash)

  • A shallow scrape of the skin surface
  • Clean thoroughly: this is the most important step. Get out all the dirt and debris. Use a brush if you have one.
  • It can be painful without anesthesia. A local numbing spray can help, if you have one.
  • Dress it with antibiotic ointment and sterile gauze

Laceration (clean cut)

  • Fairly clean wound edges
  • Irrigate and clean it thoroughly
  • Dress it and keep it clean
  • A gaping wound gets evaluated for closure at a clinic. Evacuate wounds that need closure.

Puncture wound

  • Small entrance, deep penetration
  • High infection risk: the deep environment favors anaerobic bacteria (tetanus)
  • Don't close it: closure traps infection deep in the wound
  • Encourage bleeding and drainage to flush the wound
  • Soak it in warm water if you can, to help it drain
  • If it's contaminated (soil, animal matter), they need tetanus prophylaxis

Avulsion (tissue flap)

  • Tissue torn and partly detached
  • If the tissue's still attached, return it to its normal position if you can
  • If it's completely torn off, save it in a clean, moist container. It may be reattachable.
  • Irrigate the wound and cover it loosely. Don't close it tightly.
  • Urgent evacuation for surgical evaluation

Crush wound

  • Tissue crushed by blunt force
  • Contaminated, with a high infection risk
  • Don't close it: the infection risk is high
  • Clean and dress it, and watch for signs of infection

Wound irrigation: the most important step

The idea: clean wounds are less likely to get infected

  • Volume: at least 500 mL to 1 L for dirty wounds. Keep going until the wound is visibly clean.
  • Pressure: moderate pressure works best. Use a 20-60 mL syringe, or a squeeze bottle with a pinhole in the cap.
  • Solution: clean drinking water works. Sterile saline is ideal if you have it.
  • What not to use: don't irrigate the wound bed with hydrogen peroxide or iodine. They damage tissue and kill the cells that do the healing.
  • Scrubbing: clean gently with gauze or a brush to get debris out. Avoid hard scrubbing that damages tissue.
Water beats antiseptic. Irrigating with clean water prevents infection better than any antiseptic solution.

Wounds that need closure

In the field: closing a wound is a clinical decision made at a clinic or hospital, not out here. Your job is to clean the wound thoroughly, dress it, keep it clean, and evacuate the wounds that need closure. A clinician will evaluate a gaping wound for closure. The suture kit someone packed can stay packed.

Red flags. These wounds always need to be seen by a clinician:

  • Bite wounds (dog, cat, human): high infection risk
  • Puncture wounds: infection risk from anaerobic bacteria
  • Heavily contaminated wounds (soil, feces)
  • Older wounds, many hours after the injury
  • Wounds already showing signs of infection

Dressing

  • Sterile gauze: cover the wound with clean or sterile gauze
  • Non-adherent: non-stick gauze won't disturb healing tissue when you change the dressing
  • Cover completely: cover the whole wound to keep dirt out
  • Change frequency: daily, or whenever it's wet or soiled
  • Observation: check for signs of infection at every dressing change

Infection signs and evacuation

  • Cellulitis: redness spreading past the wound edges, warmth, increasing pain
  • Purulent discharge: pus draining from the wound
  • Lymphangitis: red streaks running from the wound toward the heart. It's serious, and it indicates systemic infection.
  • Fever: a sign of systemic infection
  • Swollen lymph nodes: near the wound, like the armpit for an arm wound or the groin for a leg wound
Red streaks from a wound (lymphangitis) mean urgent evacuation. The infection is traveling toward the heart.

Tetanus and animal bites

  • Tetanus risk: any wound contaminated with soil, feces, or animal matter. Check the patient's vaccination status.
  • Booster needed if: more than 5 years since the last tetanus shot for a dirty wound, or more than 10 years for a clean one
  • Animal bites: wash immediately and aggressively. The infection risk is high (Pasteurella bacteria). A rabies risk assessment is required, and the bite gets reported to public health.
  • Don't close animal bites: even a "minor" bite stays open, because of the infection risk

Internal Bleeding

Recognizing internal bleeding

The problem: you can't see internal bleeding, but it can cause massive blood loss

  • Abdominal signs:
    • A rigid, board-like abdomen (a sign of peritoneal irritation)
    • Distension (swelling)
    • Bruising over the abdomen
    • Grey-Turner sign: bruising on the flanks (sides)
    • Cullen's sign: bruising around the umbilicus (belly button)
    • Grey-Turner and Cullen's signs are delayed findings that take hours to appear, so not seeing them early doesn't rule out bleeding
  • Other signs:
    • Blood in the stool (dark and tarry means upper GI bleeding, bright red means lower GI)
    • Blood in the urine
    • Vomiting blood (hematemesis)
  • Shock without external bleeding: think internal bleeding. Signs: pale, cool, sweaty (diaphoretic) skin; a rapid pulse; weak pulses; confusion.

Field treatment of internal bleeding

  • Position: flat on the back (supine). If the bleeding came from trauma, don't raise the legs. With no trauma, like a GI bleed, raising the feet 6 to 12 inches is an option if it doesn't hurt (Lesson 5).
  • Keep warm: prevent hypothermia, which makes shock worse
  • Nothing by mouth: they may need surgery, and keeping them NPO (nothing by mouth) reduces the aspiration risk
  • Urgent evacuation: no field treatment will stop internal bleeding. Get them to a hospital.
  • Monitor: watch for deterioration. If they're unstable, repeat vitals every 5 minutes.

Specific internal bleeding scenarios

Aortic rupture or aneurysm

  • Signs: severe tearing pain in the back or abdomen, shock, an elderly patient
  • Prognosis: extremely high mortality, even with surgery
  • Treatment: immediate evacuation, by helicopter if one is available

Pelvic fracture

  • The pelvis has large blood vessels, so a fracture can cause massive internal bleeding
  • Your actions: suspect it from the mechanism: a high fall, a crush, a high-energy impact. Do not press on or "stress" the pelvis. Keep the patient still. Evacuate emergently.
  • What EMS may do: EMS providers may apply a pelvic binder to stabilize the pelvis and reduce bleeding

Burns

On motorcycle and river trips, burns come with the territory: a bare calf against an exhaust header, a camp stove that flares, a spilled pot of boiling water, a full day of sun on the water. Most you can manage in the field. The skill is cooling them properly, dressing them simply, and recognizing the ones that have to be evacuated.

Stop the burning

  • Remove from the source: put out flames (stop, drop, and roll if their clothing is on fire), and pull them away from the exhaust pipe, stove, or fire. Get sunburned patients into shade.
  • Get jewelry and loose clothing off early: rings, watches, and bracelets come off before swelling starts. A ring on a burned finger becomes a tourniquet. Take off boots, socks, and any clothing around the burn that isn't stuck to it.
  • Never peel off melted or stuck fabric: synthetic riding gear and fleece melt into the burn. Cool over it and cut around it. Leave the stuck material in place for the clinician.

Cool the burn

  • Cool running water, 10-20 minutes: cool, not ice-cold, water as soon as you can. It's the single most effective burn treatment, because it limits how deep the burn goes.
  • Still worth doing up to ~3 hours after injury: if it takes time to reach water, cool the burn anyway when you can.
  • No ice or ice water: ice constricts blood flow and makes the injury deeper.
  • Solution: clean drinking water is ideal. On the river, though, cool clear water beats no cooling.
Cool the burn, not the patient. Cooling a large burn can chill the whole person. Keep the rest of them warm and dry, and watch for hypothermia.

Assessing the burn: depth and extent

Superficial

  • Red, dry, and painful, with no blisters. Classic sunburn.
  • Heals on its own. Comfort care only.

Partial-thickness

  • Blisters, a moist or weeping surface, and very painful
  • The typical scald, stove flare, or brief flame contact

Full-thickness

  • Leathery, waxy, or charred. The center may be painless because the nerve endings are destroyed, even when the edges hurt.
  • Always an evacuation, whatever the size
Depth shows itself over 24-48 hours. A burn often looks shallower on day one than it really is. Reassess it, and when in doubt, assume it's deeper. Extent: the patient's palm plus fingers is about 1% of their body surface area (the palm method).

Dressing the burn

  • After cooling, cover loosely: a clean, non-stick dressing over the burn. Clean plastic wrap laid on top (not wrapped all the way around) works well, and clinicians can inspect the burn without pulling the dressing off.
  • Leave blisters intact: the blister is a sterile biological dressing. Don't pop it.
  • No home remedies: no butter, toothpaste, oils, or ointments on the burn. They trap heat and cause infection. Save the butter for dinner.
  • Sunburn exception: plain aloe or moisturizer is fine, for superficial sunburn only
  • Pain control: OTC pain medication (ibuprofen). Burns hurt, so treat the pain.
  • Tetanus: burns are tetanus-prone wounds. Check vaccination status just as you would for a dirty wound (see Tetanus and animal bites in the Wound Care tab).

Burn evacuation criteria

  • Any full-thickness burn, even a small one
  • Partial-thickness burns larger than a few palm-areas (~5% of body surface)
  • Burns to the face, hands, feet, genitals, or across joints. These are high-function areas that scar badly.
  • Circumferential burns (all the way around a limb or the chest). Burned skin tightens as it swells and can cut off circulation or restrict breathing. This is a tightening emergency.
  • Electrical burns. The internal injury is far worse than the skin suggests.
  • Very young or very old patients
Airway or inhalation involvement means an emergent evacuation. A burned face, singed nasal hair or eyebrows, soot in the mouth or sputum, hoarseness, or a persistent cough after fire or smoke exposure means the airway may be burned. A burned airway can swell shut over hours. Do not wait for breathing trouble to start.

Exhaust pipes and road rash: the crossovers

  • Exhaust-pipe burns are deeper than they look: a second of contact with metal at several hundred degrees makes a small but often deep partial- or full-thickness burn. Small doesn't mean minor. Assess the depth honestly and reassess at 24-48 hours.
  • Road rash is an abrasion: manage it with aggressive cleaning and irrigation, as covered under Abrasion in the Wound Care tab
  • Large-area road rash thinks like a burn: when road rash covers a large area, think of it as a burn for fluid loss, infection risk, and evacuation decisions

Case Scenarios

Scenario 1: Rock climbing fall with arterial bleeding

Setting: A climber falls 15 feet onto rock. There's a large laceration on the left thigh with bright red, pulsatile bleeding. The patient is conscious, in pain, and in shock. You're 2 hours from the nearest road.

What you do now:

  • Bright red, pulsatile bleeding in a patient getting shocky is obviously life-threatening hemorrhage. Put a tourniquet on now, 2-3 inches above the wound. Don't wait to see if direct pressure works.
  • Press directly on the wound with a gloved hand, but only while the tourniquet is being readied
  • Tighten until the bleeding stops and the pulse at the ankle is gone. If the first tourniquet doesn't stop it, put a second one on just above or beside the first.
  • Write the application time on the skin or the tourniquet. Don't remove or loosen it in the field.
  • Once the bleeding's controlled, do a neurovascular check below the wound

About the tourniquet: It's safe in place for at least 2 hours. For obviously life-threatening bleeding from a limb, the tourniquet is the first move. Direct pressure is for bleeding that isn't clearly life-threatening, or to buy time while the tourniquet is readied.

Shock: The patient is showing signs of shock (pale, sweaty). Lay them flat on their back, prevent heat loss with insulation, treat the cause (the bleeding's now controlled), and evacuate. This is trauma, so don't raise the legs.

Evacuation: Urgent. Activate emergency services. Keep checking vitals every 5 minutes throughout the evacuation.

Scenario 2: Backpacker with a rigid abdomen and shock

Setting: A backpacker falls hard on rocks 4 miles into a wilderness area. She has abdominal pain, and you can't see any external bleeding. On assessment her abdomen is rigid, she's pale and clammy, and she's deteriorating fast. There's no sign of external bleeding anywhere.

What's going on: A rigid abdomen plus shock with no external bleeding means internal bleeding from abdominal trauma, likely from a solid organ like the spleen or liver.

Field treatment:

  • Lay her flat on her back. Don't raise the legs.
  • Keep her warm and prevent hypothermia
  • Nothing by mouth
  • Watch closely for deterioration

Evacuation: Urgent. This is a surgical emergency, and no field treatment will stop the bleeding. Immediate helicopter evacuation if one is available. If not, the fastest evacuation possible, which may mean a litter carry.

Scenario 3: Exhaust-pipe burn at the lunch stop

Setting: On a dual-sport ride, a rider gets off awkwardly at a rocky pull-out and presses her calf against the exhaust header. The burn is small, about half a palm-area. It has a pale, waxy center, a red blistering rim, and a patch of melted riding-pant fabric stuck to one edge. She says it "barely hurts" and wants to keep riding.

What you do now:

  • Cool the burn with cool, not ice-cold, water for 10-20 minutes. It still works up to about 3 hours after the injury. No ice.
  • Don't peel off the melted fabric. Cool over it, cut around it, and leave the stuck material in place.
  • Take the boot and sock off that leg before swelling starts
  • After cooling: a loose, clean, non-stick dressing. Leave the blisters intact. Ibuprofen for pain.

The red flag: A pale, waxy center that "barely hurts" suggests a full-thickness burn, because the nerve endings are destroyed. Here, "barely hurts" is the bad news. Exhaust-pipe burns are deeper than they look, and depth often shows itself over 24-48 hours. Small doesn't make this minor.

Evacuation: Any suspected full-thickness burn is an evacuation, even a small one. She rides pillion, or the group reroutes to get her to care. She does not "keep riding and see how it feels tomorrow." Burns are also tetanus-prone, so check her vaccination status.

Glossary

Hemorrhage
Bleeding: blood lost from the blood vessels.
Tourniquet
A device or material put on above a wound on an arm or leg to stop the bleeding by compression.
Hemostasis
Bleeding stopping as a blood clot forms.
Wound Packing
Gauze packed tightly into a wound to control junctional hemorrhage.
Hemostatic Gauze
Gauze impregnated with clotting agents (kaolin, chitosan) that help blood clot.
Laceration
A clean cut or tear in the skin with defined edges.
Avulsion
Tissue torn away from the body, partly or completely.
Cellulitis
Spreading inflammation and infection of the skin and the tissue around it.
Lymphangitis
Infection of the lymphatic vessels, seen as red streaks running from a wound toward the heart. It's serious and needs urgent treatment.
Exsanguination
Massive blood loss that leads to death.
Direct Pressure
Firm, continuous pressure right on the wound to control bleeding.
Pressure Dressing
An elastic bandage over gauze that holds constant pressure once the bleeding is controlled.
Partial-Thickness Burn
A burn through the epidermis into the dermis. Blistered, moist, and very painful.
Full-Thickness Burn
A burn that destroys all the skin layers. Leathery, waxy, or charred, and it may be painless in the center, where the nerve endings are destroyed.
Palm Method
Estimating a burn's extent by counting the patient's palm plus fingers as roughly 1% of their body surface area.
