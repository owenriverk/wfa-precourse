Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you'll be able to:

  • Describe what a Wilderness First Aider does, and where your scope of practice ends
  • Use Standard Precautions with whatever PPE you've got in the backcountry
  • Size up a scene for hazards before you go near any patient
  • Get informed consent, and know when implied consent applies
  • Know what Good Samaritan laws protect, and what you need to write down
📋 Don't become the second patient
Everything else in emergency response sits on your own safety. A rescuer who gets hurt helps no one. Check the scene, glove up, then help.

What is Wilderness First Aid?

Wilderness First Aid (WFA) trains you to recognize and manage medical emergencies in remote, austere places, where advanced medical care can be 30 minutes to several days away.

🏥 Urban EMS

  • ALS backup minutes away
  • Stabilize and hand off
  • Full equipment on hand
  • Strict protocols
  • Transport < 20 minutes

🏔 Wilderness First Aid

  • Help may be hours or days away
  • You assess and treat, start to finish
  • Improvise with what's in the packs
  • Decisions run on judgment
  • Long stretches of monitoring

Your Role as a WFA Provider

Knowing your scope of practice protects you and your patient. Wilderness medicine training comes in levels:

Training LevelHoursKey Capabilities
WFA16–20 hrsBasic assessment, bleeding control, splinting, environmental emergencies, evacuation decisions
WFR70–80 hrsEverything in WFA, plus giving medications, spine clearing, advanced improvisation, extended care
WEMT120+ hrsEverything in WFR, plus EMT skills adapted to the wilderness
Wilderness MDMedical degree + trainingFull scope of medical practice in remote settings
⚠️ Stay inside your training
Work within your level of training. Don't try skills nobody trained you for. When in doubt, do less, monitor more, and evacuate.

Standard Precautions

Standard Precautions means treating all blood and body fluids as potentially infectious. That's the current term; you'll also hear the older one, Body Substance Isolation (BSI). Out here your PPE may be limited. Use what you have.

🧤 Gloves (essential)

Always the first priority. Non-latex nitrile gloves are preferred. No gloves? Use plastic bags, extra clothing, anything that puts a barrier between you and body fluids.

👓 Eye protection

If splashes are possible, sunglasses work as eye protection. They matter most when you're controlling arterial bleeding.

😷 Face protection

A commercial CPR face shield, or a pocket mask with a one-way valve, is the real barrier. Clothing and fabric are not fluid barriers. If you have no barrier and can't or won't give breaths, compression-only CPR is acceptable (AHA).

Taking gloves off

  1. Grasp the outside of one glove at the wrist and peel it off, turning it inside out
  2. Hold that glove in your still-gloved hand
  3. Slide two fingers inside the other glove and peel it off inside out, over the first one
  4. Put both gloves together in a waste bag
  5. Wash your hands well, or use hand sanitizer (at least 60% alcohol)
✓ Cleaning up without a sink
No running water? Use hand sanitizer if you have it. If anything from a wound gets on your own skin, wash it with clean water and soap as soon as you can. A significant bloodborne exposure is a reason on its own to evacuate and get urgent medical evaluation. That means a needlestick or other puncture, a splash to the eyes, mouth, or nose, or blood on broken (non-intact) skin. Post-exposure treatment is time-sensitive: HIV post-exposure prophylaxis works best started within hours, and it has to begin within 72 hours.

Scene Size-Up

Before you approach any patient, do a quick scene size-up. It takes 5–15 seconds, less time than most people spend finding their headlamp, and it can save your life.

The size-up doesn't end when you reach the patient. Hazards change and the number of patients can grow, so keep reassessing for the whole response. On a fire ground, somebody is always watching the roof. Out here, that somebody is you.

The scene size-up checklist

S
Safety — Is it safe for me to go in? Are hazards still active? Anything else that could hurt me?
S
Scene — What happened? How many patients? Any witnesses?
M
Mechanism — What caused the injury? High energy or low?
H
Help — Do I need to call for help now? Can I handle this alone?
P
Protection — Do I have the right PPE on (Standard Precautions)?

Types of Scene Hazards

⚡ Environmental

  • Lightning (ongoing storm)
  • Avalanche zones
  • Swift water
  • Unstable terrain
  • Extreme heat or cold

🐻 Wildlife

  • The animal is still there
  • A venomous snake near the patient
  • Agitated wildlife
  • A bee or wasp nest nearby

👥 Human factors

  • Unstable group members
  • Hostile bystanders
  • Fire or toxic smoke
  • Moving vehicles or boats

⬆️ Structural

  • Falling rocks or debris
  • Unstable structures
  • Vertical exposure
  • Anchors that might not hold
🚨 Never enter an unsafe scene
A rescuer who becomes a second victim doubles the problem. If the scene isn't safe and can't be made safe, set up a safe perimeter, call for specialized rescue, and wait. Pressure from bystanders to "do something" doesn't make an unsafe scene safe.

Mechanism of Injury (MOI)

The mechanism of injury tells you what forces hit the patient's body. That lets you predict injuries you can't see yet. A high-energy MOI calls for a more thorough assessment.

High-Energy MOILow-Energy MOI
Fall >3× patient's heightSimple trip and fall
High-speed vehicle crashLow-speed ground-level fall
Direct high-force impactOveruse or repetitive injury
Diving into waterMinor blunt force
Explosive blastSymptoms that came on gradually

Documentation: The SOAP Note

The SOAP note is the standard way to write up a patient in wilderness medicine. You'll use it all through the course.

SOAP note format

S
Subjective — What the patient tells you: chief complaint, history, symptoms. The SAMPLE mnemonic (Signs/Symptoms, Allergies, Medications, Past history, Last oral intake, Events) is a quick way to gather that history. You'll learn it properly in Lesson 2.
O
Objective — What you find: vital signs, physical exam findings, level of consciousness (LOC)
A
Assessment — Your field impression: what do you think is wrong?
P
Plan — Treatment you gave, the evacuation decision, and how you'll keep monitoring

Consent in Emergency Care

You need consent before you treat anyone. Depending on the jurisdiction, treating someone without it can count as assault, battery, or both.

📢 Informed consent

Who: Conscious, alert adults who can make their own decisions

How: Say who you are, your training, and what you want to do, then ask permission: "My name is Alex, I'm a Wilderness First Aider. I'd like to assess your injury. Is that okay?"

What: They have to understand what's going on and agree to treatment

🔇 Implied consent

Who: Unconscious patients, and patients whose mental status is severely altered

How: The law assumes a reasonable person in their situation would consent to life-saving treatment

What: Go ahead with the care they need, without a spoken yes

👶 Pediatric consent

Who: Patients under 18 (minors, in most states)

How: Ideally, a parent or guardian consents. If you can't reach one in an emergency, implied consent applies.

What: Always document every attempt to reach a guardian

🚫 Refusal of care

A conscious, competent adult has the right to refuse treatment. If they do:

  • Make sure they understand the risks
  • Document the refusal
  • Make sure they aren't altered (drugs, head injury)
  • Keep monitoring them if it's safe to

Good Samaritan Laws

Most jurisdictions have laws that protect lay rescuers who give emergency care in good faith. The protection generally depends on four things:

  • You act in good faith to help someone in an emergency
  • You don't act with gross negligence or willful misconduct
  • You act within your level of training
  • You don't take payment for the care

Related, but a separate legal concept: once you start care, you have to stay and help until someone of equal or higher training relieves you, or the patient is in better hands. Leaving before that is abandonment, covered below.

⚠️ Check your local law
Good Samaritan laws vary a lot by state and country. This course gives general guidance, so always check your local laws. Your best legal protection is thorough documentation and staying within your scope of training.

Duty to Act & Abandonment

If you're a WFA provider with no professional role, your duty to respond is generally moral, not legal, unless your employer or role requires it (a guide, for example). Once you start caring for a patient, though, you must not abandon them. Abandonment is when:

  • You leave a patient worse off than you found them, or no better
  • You stop care without handing off to someone of equal or greater training
  • You leave without the patient's informed consent to stop care

Clinical Scenarios

Two wilderness situations to work through. Read each one, decide what you'd do, then open the discussion.

🏔 Scenario 1: The Fallen Hiker

Setting: You're hiking a steep trail when someone cries out for help. A hiker is lying 30 feet off the trail at the bottom of a rocky slope, with loose rock above. They look conscious, and they're holding their leg. No one else is around.

Questions to consider:

  • What do you do first, before you approach the patient?
  • What hazards do you see?
  • What BSI precautions can you take with what's in your day pack?
  • What do you need to know to decide whether to call for help now?

1. Size up the scene first. Before you take a step toward the patient, stop and look at the slope above. Is there more loose rock up there? Could more come down where the patient is lying? Find the most sheltered way in.

2. Hazards. Loose rock on the slope above (an ongoing hazard), unstable ground on the way in, the chance you fall and get hurt crossing it, and isolation, with no other rescuers on scene.

3. BSI. Gloves on from your pack. No gloves? A clean bandana or a plastic bag over your hands will do for first contact, but any substantial wound care needs a barrier.

4. Calling for help. Weigh how bad the injury looks (can they walk?), the distance to the trailhead, how much daylight is left, what the group has, and whether you have a device to call out on. If the mechanism looks significant, like a fall of 10+ feet onto rock, call sooner rather than later.

Look up before you go in. Rushing to the patient is the most natural impulse, and the most dangerous one. The scene size-up isn't optional.

⛺ Scenario 2: Consent Complication

Setting: You're a camp counselor. A 15-year-old camper has a significant cut (laceration) on their arm from a sharp rock. It's bleeding steadily, but pressure is controlling it. The camper says, "I'm fine, leave me alone, don't touch me." They're alert. They seem upset, but not confused.

Questions to consider:

  • Is this refusal valid? Can they legally refuse care?
  • What do you do next?
  • When would implied consent apply here?

A minor's refusal. A 15-year-old is a minor in virtually every jurisdiction. Respect their feelings and don't get forceful, but a minor generally can't refuse medical care on their own. That authority usually sits with their parents or guardians. Rules vary by state, and in some places emancipated or "mature minors" are exceptions. Organized programs like camps and guided trips usually keep parental consent-to-treat forms on file, so know your program's policies.

What to do:

  • Stay calm and empathetic. Ask, "Can you tell me why you don't want me to help?"
  • Contact the parents or guardians and the camp director right away
  • Document what the patient says and what you do
  • If the bleeding becomes uncontrolled or the patient loses consciousness, implied consent lets you treat
  • Never use force. Get the adults in charge involved.

When implied consent kicks in: the patient becomes unconscious or severely altered, or the injury turns immediately life-threatening. At that point you treat under implied consent while you get help.

🎯 The short version

  • Your safety comes first, every time. Never enter an unsafe scene.
  • Standard Precautions (formerly BSI): treat all blood and body fluids as potentially infectious, and use whatever barriers you have.
  • Scene size-up: Safety, Scene, Mechanism, Help needed, Protection (PPE). Then keep reassessing.
  • A high-energy MOI predicts hidden injuries. Always do a thorough assessment.
  • Get informed consent from conscious adults. Implied consent covers the unconscious.
  • Once you start care, you can't abandon the patient.
  • Document everything in a SOAP note.

Glossary

Standard Precautions
Precautions against exposure to any patient's blood and body fluids, on the assumption that all of it is potentially infectious. You'll also hear the older term, Body Substance Isolation (BSI).
Scene Size-Up
A quick assessment before you approach a patient: spot the hazards, count the patients, work out the mechanism of injury, and make sure you're safe.
Informed Consent
A conscious, competent adult agreeing to treatment after you've told them who you are, your training level, and what you plan to do.
Implied Consent
The legal assumption that an unconscious or severely impaired patient would consent to life-saving care if they could.
Good Samaritan Law
Legal protection for lay rescuers who give emergency care in good faith, without gross negligence, and without expecting payment.
MOI (Mechanism of Injury)
The forces and circumstances behind a patient's injury. You use it to predict injuries that haven't shown up yet.
SOAP Note
The standard format for writing up a patient: Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan. It records what you found and what you did.
Abandonment
Illegally stopping care for a patient without handing responsibility to an equal or higher-level provider, or without the patient's informed agreement.
