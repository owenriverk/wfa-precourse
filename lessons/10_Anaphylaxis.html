Overview: Allergic & Anaphylactic Reactions

Allergies are immune reactions to something foreign. Most are minor, like itching or hives. Some turn life-threatening fast. The range runs from a local reaction at the sting site to anaphylactic shock that needs epinephrine immediately.

Epinephrine first, not last. Anaphylaxis is a medical emergency, and epinephrine must go in immediately.

Key points

Anaphylaxis is a severe allergic reaction that comes on fast and hits more than one body system.
Epinephrine is the first-line treatment for anaphylaxis. Diphenhydramine (Benadryl) isn't.
Common wilderness triggers: bee, wasp, and fire ant stings, food allergies, and medications.
Anaphylaxis can come back (biphasic anaphylaxis). Most second waves hit within the first hours, but some come up to 72 hours later. All anaphylaxis evacuates.
Time matters. Symptoms build in minutes, and treatment saves lives.

Allergic Reaction Spectrum

How the reaction works

  • IgE-mediated: Immunoglobulin E antibodies attach to mast cells. When the allergen arrives, the mast cells break open (degranulate) and release histamine and other mediators.
  • What histamine does:
    • Makes blood vessels leak, so tissue swells (edema)
    • Makes vessel walls more permeable
    • Narrows the airways (bronchoconstriction)
    • Widens the blood vessels (vasodilation)
    • Sets off itching and hives

Mild (localized) allergic reaction

  • Signs: Skin only, at or near the sting or contact site: hives (urticaria), itching, mild redness (erythema). Nothing anywhere else in the body.
  • Example: A mosquito bite that itches and raises a small bump
  • Treatment:
    • Diphenhydramine (Benadryl) 25-50 mg by mouth
    • Hydrocortisone cream on the skin for itch
    • Ice or a cool compress
  • Monitoring: Watch closely for at least 30 minutes and keep rechecking. Look for spread beyond the site or new symptoms in another body system. Progression means anaphylaxis.

More than skin-only = anaphylaxis

  • Treat as anaphylaxis if any of these follow a likely allergen exposure:
    • Any lower-airway involvement: wheezing, chest or throat tightness, trouble breathing
    • Signs in 2 or more body systems (for example, hives plus nausea, or hives plus swelling of the lips, tongue, or face)
    • Any cardiovascular sign: dizziness, faintness, a weak pulse, collapse
  • Treatment:
    • Epinephrine immediately. Don't wait to see if it gets worse.
    • Antihistamines (diphenhydramine 25-50 mg) only after epinephrine, as an add-on for skin symptoms. They don't treat anaphylaxis.

Severe (anaphylaxis)

Definition: A severe allergic reaction that comes on fast and involves 2 or more body systems, or a single life-threatening reaction

  • Criteria for anaphylaxis:
    • Skin involvement (hives or flushing) plus either breathing symptoms (wheezing, stridor, shortness of breath) or cardiovascular symptoms (low blood pressure, dizziness, fainting)
    • Or breathing or cardiovascular symptoms alone, after known allergen exposure
  • This is a medical emergency.

Anaphylaxis Recognition

Signs and symptoms

  • Skin: Hives (urticaria) all over the body, flushing, intense itching (pruritus)
  • Respiratory:
    • Bronchospasm: wheezing, shortness of breath, chest tightness
    • Upper airway swelling (edema): stridor (high-pitched breathing), voice changes, trouble swallowing
    • Shortness of breath (dyspnea)
  • Cardiovascular:
    • Low blood pressure (hypotension): dizziness, fainting (syncope), shock
    • Fast heart rate (tachycardia)
    • Chest pain or palpitations
  • GI: Nausea, vomiting, cramping, diarrhea
  • Neurologic: Anxiety, confusion, a sense of impending doom

Time course

  • Onset: Usually within minutes of exposure, up to 1 hour
  • The faster the onset, the more severe the reaction.
    • Symptoms within 5 minutes: severe and life-threatening
    • Symptoms at 15-30 minutes: more time to intervene
  • Why speed matters: A swelling airway can block breathing, and shock builds quickly. This is urgent.

Common triggers in the wilderness

  • Insect stings (the most common):
    • Hymenoptera: bees, wasps, hornets
    • Fire ants
    • Anaphylaxis risk: about 1% of people stung
  • Food: Peanuts, tree nuts, shellfish, eggs, milk
  • Medications: Penicillin, NSAIDs, aspirin
  • Latex: Gloves (yours included) and other equipment
  • Venom: Snake venom (rare)

Biphasic anaphylaxis

  • What it is: A second wave of anaphylaxis after the first reaction settles. Most come within the first hours, but some come up to 72 hours later.
  • How often: 5-20% of anaphylaxis cases
  • Why: More mast cells break open, or the allergen keeps acting.
  • What it means for you: Every anaphylaxis patient must be evacuated and monitored in a hospital, because nobody can predict who'll get a second wave. It's a rekindle. A knocked-down fire can flare back hours later, which is why a crew stays for overhaul. The hospital is overhaul.

EpiPen (Epinephrine Auto-Injector) Administration

Your job as a wilderness first aider is to help the patient use their own prescribed epinephrine auto-injector. The device and prescription are theirs, and you're assisting with their medication. Many states also protect trained laypeople who give epinephrine in an emergency, so know the rules where you live and travel.

How epinephrine works

  • Alpha-1 effects: Vasoconstriction, raising blood pressure and reversing shock
  • Beta-1 effects: Faster, stronger heartbeat (rate and contractility), improving cardiac output
  • Beta-2 effects: Bronchodilation, opening the airways and reversing bronchospasm
  • Net result: Reverses the hallmark signs of anaphylaxis: low blood pressure, bronchospasm, deep swelling (angioedema)

EpiPen dosing

  • Adult auto-injector: 0.3 mg
  • EpiPen Jr (pediatric): 0.15 mg, for children about 15-30 kg (33-66 lbs)
  • Smaller children, under 15 kg: 0.15 mg or specialty pediatric dosing

Injection technique

  • Site: The outer side of the thigh, halfway between hip and knee (lateral mid-thigh)
  • Why the thigh: Plenty of muscle for absorption, easy to reach (even through pants), away from vital structures
  • Steps:
    • Pull off the blue safety release cap at the top.
    • Hold it firmly, orange tip (the needle end) pointing down.
    • Swing and push the orange tip firmly against the outer thigh at 90°, straight in.
    • Hold in place for 3 seconds (per current auto-injector labeling).
    • Remove it. Massaging the site is no longer recommended.
  • Through clothing is fine: Denim and other fabric won't stop the needle. Leave the pants on.
Learn it before you need it. Know how to use your EpiPen ahead of time, and carry a trainer (practice auto-injector) on trips to review technique.

Onset and duration

  • Onset: 5-15 minutes
  • Duration: 20-30 minutes
  • Can repeat: Every 5-15 minutes if there's no improvement or symptoms return
  • Carry more than one: Anyone with known anaphylaxis risk should carry at least 2 EpiPens

Side effects

  • Palpitations (feeling the heart pound)
  • Tremor and shakiness
  • Headache and anxiety
  • These are expected and temporary. Far better than dying of anaphylaxis.

Anaphylaxis Field Management

Treatment priority: epinephrine first

Epi before Benadryl. Give epinephrine before diphenhydramine (Benadryl). Don't hold it for any other medication.
  • Right away: Epinephrine IM (into the muscle of the thigh)
  • Then follow with:
    • Oral diphenhydramine 25-50 mg for skin symptoms
    • Help the patient use their own inhaler for wheezing that won't quit. After epinephrine, never instead of it.

Positioning

  • Breathing trouble: Sitting upright, which makes breathing easier
  • Low blood pressure or shock: Flat on their back (supine). Raising the legs may help if breathing isn't impaired. This is anaphylaxis-specific: blood pools when the patient stands.
  • Nausea or vomiting risk: Recovery position (on their side) if unconscious
  • Never stand or sit the patient up abruptly. In anaphylaxis, a sudden position change can cause fatal collapse.

Field management, start to finish

  1. Recognize anaphylaxis. Don't wait for "confirmation."
  2. Give epinephrine IM immediately (0.3 mg adult, 0.15 mg child).
  3. Position the patient: upright for breathing trouble, flat for shock.
  4. Give supplemental oxygen if you have it.
  5. Follow with diphenhydramine for skin symptoms.
  6. Watch vital signs and the airway the whole time.
  7. Urgent evacuation. All anaphylaxis needs hospital evaluation.

Watching for a second wave

  • Even a patient who bounces back after epinephrine can relapse, most often within the first hours but possibly up to 72 hours later.
  • No field observation is enough. They need hospital monitoring.
  • Evacuate them even if they feel fine.

What not to do

  • Don't give fluids by mouth if the airway is swelling. It's an aspiration risk.
  • Don't rely on antihistamines alone to stop anaphylaxis. They won't.
  • Don't delay the evacuation because the patient "feels better" after epinephrine.
  • Don't refuse epinephrine over its side effects. Dying is worse.

Case Scenarios

Scenario 1: Bee sting with anaphylaxis on the trail

Setting: A hiker is stung by multiple bees. Within 5 minutes she's wheezing, hives are spreading across her body, and her lips are swelling. She's getting anxious and short of breath. Her EpiPen is in the group first aid kit.

Recognition: Anaphylaxis: a sting, then fast-onset breathing (wheezing) and skin (hives) involvement.

Immediate actions:

  • Get her EpiPen out of the first aid kit.
  • Pull the blue cap and point the orange tip at her thigh.
  • Inject into the outer thigh muscle. Through the pants is fine.
  • Hold for 3 seconds, then remove.
  • Sit her upright for the respiratory distress.
  • Watch the airway, and be ready to manage it if the swelling worsens.

Follow-up:

  • Diphenhydramine if you have it, after epinephrine, for skin symptoms.
  • Help her use her own inhaler if the wheezing continues. After epinephrine, never instead of it.
  • Urgent evacuation: activate emergency services.
  • Watch for a biphasic reaction on the way out.

Scenario 2: Hidden food allergen at camp

Setting: A backpacker with a known peanut allergy eats a groupmate's trail mix, not realizing it has peanuts. Fifteen minutes later her throat feels tight, she's dizzy, and she's breaking out in hives. She has an EpiPen but says, "Maybe I'm overreacting."

Recognition: Anaphylaxis: known allergen exposure, then throat tightness (airway), dizziness (cardiovascular), and hives (skin). That meets the criteria.

Don't let her talk you out of it. "Maybe I'm overreacting" isn't a vital sign. Rapid progression is likely.

Management:

  • Get the EpiPen out now. Do not delay.
  • Inject into the thigh.
  • Lay her flat. Legs can go up if her breathing isn't impaired. Never let her stand or sit up abruptly.
  • Oral diphenhydramine after epinephrine, for skin symptoms only.
  • Prepare for a helicopter evacuation. Dizziness points to cardiovascular involvement.

Lesson Summary

The short version

Anaphylaxis is a severe allergic reaction that hits multiple body systems. It's life-threatening.
Epinephrine is first-line. Give it immediately, IM into the outer thigh.
EpiPen doses: adult 0.3 mg, pediatric 0.15 mg.
Common triggers: bee and wasp stings, food allergies, medications.
Onset in minutes means an emergency. Get the epinephrine in fast.

Positioning: upright for breathing trouble, flat for shock.

Glossary

Anaphylaxis
A severe allergic reaction that comes on fast and involves 2 or more body systems, or a single life-threatening reaction
Epinephrine
A hormone that reverses anaphylaxis by constricting blood vessels, opening the airways, and strengthening heart contractions
Auto-Injector
A self-injecting syringe (such as an EpiPen) that delivers a fixed dose of epinephrine into the muscle
Urticaria
Hives: raised, itchy welts on the skin from an allergic reaction
Angioedema
Swelling in the deeper layers of skin and tissue, often in the lips, tongue, and face
Bronchospasm
The airways (bronchi) clamping down, causing wheezing and shortness of breath
Biphasic Reaction
A second wave of anaphylaxis after the first reaction. Most come within the first hours, but some come up to 72 hours later.
Diphenhydramine
An antihistamine (Benadryl). In anaphylaxis it's a secondary treatment, given after epinephrine.
IgE
Immunoglobulin E, an antibody involved in allergic reactions
Allergen
A foreign substance that sets off an allergic reaction
Histamine
A chemical mast cells release in an allergic reaction. It causes itching, swelling, and airway narrowing (bronchoconstriction).
