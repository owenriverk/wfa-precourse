Overview: Airway & Breathing

The airway is the patient's lifeline. Without enough oxygen, a patient dies within minutes. In the wilderness you'll recognize and manage airway and breathing emergencies with limited advanced equipment.

These kill fast. Airway and breathing problems are immediately life-threatening. You find them and manage them in the primary survey, the A and B of XABCDE.

Why it's harder out here

  • Remote location: You may not have advanced airway equipment. Positioning and manual maneuvers are your main tools.
  • Delayed evacuation: You may have to keep an airway open for hours before rescue can reach you.
  • Altitude effects: There's less oxygen at elevation, and that makes any breathing problem worse.
  • Cold/heat: Extreme cold or heat changes how fast and how hard people breathe.

Key points

In an unconscious patient, the tongue is the most common cause of airway obstruction.
A choking patient with an effective cough needs encouragement. Go hands-on only when the cough fails.
Rescue breathing is a core skill at this level. It can keep someone alive until help arrives.
Wilderness CPR can run long. Don't assume a hypothermic patient is dead.

Airway Management

Airway anatomy

Where air goes, and where it gets stuck:

  • Nose/Mouth: where air gets in
  • Pharynx: the throat. A common spot for blockage: tongue, blood, vomit, swelling (edema).
  • Larynx: the voice box. It can swell from an allergic reaction or trauma.
  • Trachea: the windpipe, the main airway to the lungs
  • Bronchi: the right and left branches, one into each lung

Common causes of obstruction

  • Tongue (most common in unconscious patients): it relaxes and falls back across the pharynx
  • Foreign body: food, a rock, dentures, vomit
  • Blood/Vomit: fluid blocks the airway
  • Swelling (edema): from an allergic reaction, infection, inhalation injury, or trauma
  • Direct injury: trauma to the neck, or direct damage to the larynx

Opening techniques

A tongue blocking the airway is a kinked hose line: straighten the line and air can move again. Both techniques below straighten it.

Head-tilt chin-lift

  • When to use: no concern for a spinal injury
  • How: one hand on the forehead, gently tilt the head back. Fingers under the chin, and lift. That lifts the tongue off the pharynx.
  • Advantage: opens the airway in most cases
  • Disadvantage: can make a neck (cervical spine) injury worse, if there is one

Jaw thrust

  • When to use: suspected spinal injury (trauma, a fall, unknown mechanism)
  • How: grip the angles of the jaw (mandible) from below and behind, and pull it forward without tilting the head. The cervical spine stays in line.
  • Advantage: opens the airway and protects the cervical spine
  • Disadvantage: harder to do, and it takes practice
Practice first. The jaw thrust is harder than the head-tilt chin-lift. Learn it on a friend on the living room floor, not a patient on a scree slope.

Recovery position

When to use: an unconscious patient who's breathing, with no concern for a spinal injury

  • Roll the patient onto their side
  • Rest the head on the extended arm to keep the airway open
  • Bend the top leg for stability
  • Why: if they vomit, it drains out of the mouth instead of going into the lungs (aspiration)
  • Monitor: watch for vomiting, keep the airway positioned, and recheck breathing

Suctioning and clearing the airway

  • Patient positioning: head down, so gravity drains the fluid
  • Finger sweep: only if you can actually see the object. Never do a blind finger sweep in an adult. You can push the blockage deeper.
  • Patient coughing: if they're conscious or semi-conscious, encourage them to cough out the fluid or foreign material
  • Suction improvisation: no suction device? Roll them onto their side and let gravity work.

Choking (foreign body airway obstruction)

A conscious patient choking on food or anything else needs a fast decision from you. Is the cough working, or not?

  • Universal choking sign: hands clutched to the throat. Recognize it and move.
  • Ask: "Are you choking? Can you speak?" The answer decides everything after it.
  • Effective cough: they can speak, cry out, or cough forcefully. Their own cough clears an obstruction better than anything you can do. Encourage the coughing, stay close, and watch. Do not step in physically.
  • Ineffective (silent) choking: can't speak, a weak cough or none, a high-pitched noise on the in-breath, turning blue (cyanosis). The airway is blocked. Act now.
Silent means minutes. A silent choking patient has minutes before they lose consciousness. If they can't speak or cough forcefully, start back blows and abdominal thrusts right away.

Relieving choking: conscious adult or child

  • 5 back blows: lean them forward and give 5 firm blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand.
  • 5 abdominal thrusts: stand behind them. Put a fist, thumb side in, just above the navel. Grab it with your other hand and pull sharply in and up.
  • Alternate: keep cycling 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts until the object comes out or they go unresponsive
  • Pregnant or very large patients: use chest thrusts instead of abdominal thrusts. Fist on the center of the breastbone, thrust straight back.
  • Choking while alone: give yourself abdominal thrusts with your own fist, or drive your upper abdomen against a firm edge like a chair back, a log, or a boulder
  • Infants (under 1 year): alternate 5 back slaps and 5 chest thrusts. Never abdominal thrusts.
Trail food is choking food. Dry jerky and hard candy, eaten on the move on a river trip or a bumpy ride, by someone laughing mid-bite. Slow down to eat, and keep an eye on the group at meal breaks.

If the choking patient goes unresponsive

  • Lower them carefully to the ground, and send someone for help if anyone else is there.
  • Start CPR, beginning with compressions. Compressions raise the pressure in the chest and may dislodge the object.
  • Look before you breathe: before each set of breaths, open the mouth and look. Take an object out only if you can see it. No blind finger sweeps, same rule as clearing the airway above.

Aftercare:

  • Medical evaluation: anyone who got abdominal thrusts needs a medical evaluation. The thrusts can cause internal injury.
  • Far from help: a partial obstruction far from help is an airway emergency. Lower your threshold for evacuation. A "mostly cleared" airway can swell or block again.

Airway adjuncts (reference only)

What EMS will do: EMS providers carry airway adjuncts, the nasopharyngeal airway (NPA) and the oropharyngeal airway (OPA), to help hold an unconscious patient's airway open. Using them is beyond the scope of this course. Your tools are positioning: head-tilt chin-lift or jaw thrust to open the airway, and the recovery position to protect it.

Breathing Emergencies

Recognizing respiratory distress

Signs a patient is struggling to breathe:

  • Rate: fast (tachypnea, over 20 a minute) or slow (bradypnea, under 10)
  • Accessory muscle use: the neck muscles (SCM) and the muscles between the ribs (intercostals) are working hard
  • Nasal flaring: the nostrils widen with each breath, a sign of effort
  • Pursed lips: they're breathing against resistance
  • Tripod position: sitting forward, braced on their hands, to bring in the accessory muscles
  • Abnormal sounds: stridor (a high-pitched sound on the in-breath), wheezing, gurgling, snoring
Silent isn't better. A silent chest (no breath sounds) in a patient in respiratory distress is extremely serious. It needs aggressive airway management immediately.

Asthma in the field

  • Common triggers: exercise, cold air, allergens, emotional stress
  • Signs: wheezing, shortness of breath, chest tightness, cough
  • Treatment:
    • Sit them upright
    • Help them use their own bronchodilator inhaler (albuterol/salbutamol): 4-10 puffs, through a spacer if they have one, repeated every 20 minutes for the first hour as needed
    • Keep them calm. Anxiety makes bronchospasm worse.
    • Oxygen, if you have it
    • Hydration: encourage sips of water
  • When to evacuate: getting worse despite treatment is an emergent evacuation. If there's no real improvement within the first hour of treatment, evacuate urgently.
  • Status asthmaticus: a severe attack that isn't responding to bronchodilators. It's life-threatening and needs urgent evacuation.

Pneumothorax (collapsed lung)

  • What it is: air gets into the space around the lung, and the lung collapses
  • Signs: breath sounds quiet or absent on one side, chest pain, shortness of breath (dyspnea)
  • Tension pneumothorax (an emergency): pressure builds and pushes on the heart and great vessels. Signs: the trachea shifts away from the affected side (tracheal deviation), bulging neck veins (JVD, jugular vein distension), low blood pressure, shock.
  • Field recognition: your job is to recognize the pattern, not to make a definitive diagnosis. Definitive treatment happens in a hospital.
  • Field treatment: position of comfort, watch the airway, oxygen if you have it, emergent evacuation

High altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE)

  • What it is: fluid fills the lungs because of pressure changes at altitude
  • When it happens: usually above 2,500 m (8,000 ft), most often on day 2 or 3 at altitude
  • Signs: cough, often with pink frothy sputum; crackles on auscultation (listening to the lungs); severe shortness of breath; more tired than everyone else
  • Risk factors: fast ascent, exertion at altitude, a previous episode of HAPE, individual susceptibility
  • Treatment: descend immediately. Even 500 m helps. Oxygen if you have it. Sit them upright. Do not delay the descent. The summit will still be there next year.

Chest injuries that affect breathing

Flail segment

  • What: multiple rib fractures leave a segment that moves opposite to the rest of the chest (paradoxical motion)
  • Signs: chest pain, shortness of breath, visible paradoxical motion
  • Treatment: pain control; position of comfort, often lying injured side down; a bulky pad held gently over the segment for support; encourage deep breathing; evacuate. Don't tape or wrap anything around the chest. Binding it all the way around restricts breathing.

Open pneumothorax (sucking chest wound)

  • What: a penetrating wound through the chest wall that lets air move in and out as they breathe
  • Signs: a hole in the chest, air noises with each breath, shortness of breath
  • Treatment: cover it with a 3-sided occlusive dressing. Tape 3 sides and leave 1 loose, so air can get out but can't get in. Watch for a tension pneumothorax developing. If breathing gets worse after you seal it, lift ("burp") the edge of the dressing to let the trapped air out. Urgent evacuation.

CPR in the Wilderness

When to start CPR

  • They're unresponsive: no response to your voice or to pain
  • They're not breathing, or only gasping (agonal respirations)
  • No pulse check. Unresponsive and not breathing means you start CPR.
Gasping doesn't count. It isn't normal breathing, and a patient who's gasping still needs CPR.

CPR technique

  • Compression rate: 100-120 compressions a minute, roughly the beat of a fast song
  • Depth: 2-2.4 inches (5-6 cm) for adults. "Push hard and fast."
  • Hand position: heel of one hand on the center of the chest, the other hand on top, arms straight
  • Full recoil: let the chest come all the way back up between compressions

CPR ratios and rescue breathing

  • Adult CPR ratio: 30 compressions to 2 breaths (30:2). It's the same with one rescuer or two, and using an AED never changes it.
  • Rescue breathing (they have a pulse but aren't breathing adequately): 1 breath every 6 seconds (10 a minute). Reassess about every 2 minutes.
  • Breath technique: mouth-to-mouth or mouth-to-barrier
    • Tilt the head back (if there's no spine concern)
    • Pinch the nose, seal over the mouth, and blow for 1 second
    • Enough volume means the chest rises with each breath
Out here, breaths matter. Hands-only CPR (nonstop compressions, no breaths) is a fallback for presumed sudden cardiac arrest when you can't or won't give breaths. It beats no CPR at all. The arrests common in the wilderness are asphyxial, though: drowning, avalanche burial, airway obstruction. The underlying problem is a lack of oxygen, so rescue breaths are essential.

When to withhold or stop CPR

When not to start CPR:

  • Obvious death: decapitation, rigor mortis, dependent lividity (purple discoloration where blood has pooled with gravity)
  • Injuries incompatible with life
  • Standing orders, or DNR (Do Not Resuscitate) documents

When to stop CPR:

  • ROSC (return of spontaneous circulation): they have a pulse and they're breathing
  • Exhaustion: you physically can't keep doing CPR
  • Prolonged cardiac arrest (generally 30 minutes) without ROSC in a patient with a normal body temperature (normothermic)
  • The exception is hypothermia or drowning. "No one is dead until warm and dead." If you suspect hypothermia or drowning, keep CPR going even after a prolonged arrest.

Hypothermia and cardiac arrest

Cold can look like death. A severely hypothermic patient may have a pulse you can't detect and look dead, and still be resuscitable. "No one is dead until warm and dead."

  • If you suspect hypothermia, keep CPR going during the evacuation
  • Rewarming in a hospital (heart-lung bypass or ECMO in severe cases) may restore life even after hours without a pulse
  • There are case reports of full recovery after cardiac arrest from extreme hypothermia, with core temperatures under 20°C (68°F)
Don't call it early. A hypothermic patient isn't dead until they're warm and dead. Keep CPR going.

AED (automated external defibrillator)

  • What it does: analyzes the heart rhythm and delivers a shock if defibrillation is indicated
  • When available: many wilderness rescue organizations carry AEDs
  • Wet patient: dry the chest before the pads go on
  • Chest hair: press the pads on firmly. Dense hair can keep them from making contact. If the AED can't analyze, quickly shave the pad sites (most AED kits carry a razor for exactly this) or pull the hair off with the first set of pads and put on a fresh set.
  • Pacemaker/ICD: place the pads clear of the implanted device if you can, but don't delay defibrillation
  • Use: turn it on, follow the prompts, and keep up CPR between shocks

Case Scenarios

Scenario 1: Unconscious drowning victim on a riverbank

Setting: A friend pulls a swimmer out of the river. The swimmer is unconscious and not breathing. You're 2 miles from the trailhead with limited supplies and a satellite communicator.

What you do now:

  • Lay them on their back on a firm surface
  • Check responsiveness and breathing, taking no more than 10 seconds
  • They're not breathing. Drowning is an asphyxial (oxygen-deprivation) arrest, so open the airway and give 2 rescue breaths first.
  • Then continue CPR at 30 compressions to 2 breaths (30:2)
  • Hands-only CPR is not appropriate for drowning. The problem is a lack of oxygen, so rescue breaths are essential.
  • Activate the satellite communicator for a SAR helicopter without stopping CPR

Water in the lungs: Drowning patients may have inhaled (aspirated) water, and CPR may not work as well. Do it anyway. CPR is the only hope.

Cold water: After a cold-water drowning, survival is possible even after a long time under. Keep CPR going. Do not stop because of how much time has passed.

Evacuation: Urgent. Keep CPR going through the evacuation. Stop only for ROSC, exhaustion, or definite signs of death.

Scenario 2: Asthma attack on a remote trail

Setting: A 35-year-old with known asthma is hiking at 9,000 ft. After a hard climb she starts wheezing and gets short of breath. She has her rescue inhaler (albuterol) and has used it, but she's getting more distressed and wheezier anyway.

What's going on:

  • Respiratory distress: wheezing, shortness of breath, fast breathing
  • She's likely using her accessory muscles
  • Anxiety, which makes the bronchospasm worse

What you do now:

  • Stop, and sit her upright
  • Help her use her own albuterol inhaler: 4-10 puffs, through a spacer if she has one, repeated every 20 minutes for the first hour as needed
  • Keep her calm. Speak slowly and reassure her.
  • Offer small sips of water
  • Watch how she responds to the medication

Evacuation decision: Even if the wheezing improves, she should rest and descend. After a significant attack, lower your threshold for evacuation. If she's getting worse despite treatment, it's an emergent evacuation. At altitude, going lower helps too, because there's more oxygen available.

Glossary

Airway
The path air takes from the nose and mouth to the lungs.
Jaw Thrust
A way to open the airway by pulling the jaw forward without tilting the head. Used when you suspect a spine injury.
Head-Tilt Chin-Lift
A way to open the airway by tilting the head back and lifting the chin. Use it when there's no spine injury.
Recovery Position
An unconscious, breathing patient placed on their side to protect the airway from aspiration.
NPA
Nasopharyngeal airway. An airway adjunct carried by EMS providers. Reference only; it's beyond the scope of this course.
OPA
Oropharyngeal airway. An airway adjunct carried by EMS providers. Reference only; it's beyond the scope of this course.
CPR
Cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Chest compressions and rescue breathing to maintain circulation and oxygenation.
FBAO
Foreign body airway obstruction, or "choking": an object, food, or debris blocking the airway. In a conscious patient, you relieve it by alternating 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts.
Abdominal Thrusts
Quick in-and-up thrusts just above the navel to relieve severe choking (also called the Heimlich maneuver). Anyone who gets them needs a medical evaluation afterward.
Pneumothorax
A collapsed lung, from air getting into the pleural space.
ROSC
Return of spontaneous circulation. The patient has a detectable pulse during CPR.
Stridor
A high-pitched sound on the in-breath that points to an upper airway obstruction.
Apnea
Not breathing.
Tachypnea
Fast breathing. In an adult, over 20 breaths a minute.
