Bites, Stings & Envenomation

Managing animal and insect injuries in the wilderness

Lesson Overview

Learning objectives

  • Assess an animal bite and care for the wound in the right order
  • Recognize and manage venomous snakebite, from pit vipers and from coral snakes
  • Treat stings from bees, wasps, and their relatives (hymenoptera) and spider bites, and tell neurotoxic from cytotoxic presentations
  • Remove a tick properly and recognize tick-borne illness
  • Manage marine envenomation from jellyfish, stingrays, and sea urchins

Why this lesson matters

Most bites are minor. The ones that aren't, like a rattlesnake or a bat bite nobody noticed, punish guesswork. The job is knowing which is which.

Envenomation in the wilderness

North America's list of animals that can genuinely hurt you is short. Don't handle them, and most of this lesson stays theoretical. Envenomation is venom injected into tissue, and what it does depends heavily on the species. Hemotoxic venoms (pit vipers) destroy tissue around the bite and disrupt clotting. Neurotoxic venoms (coral snakes, some spiders) go after the nervous system. Knowing what bit them, and which kind of venom effect you're looking at, guides your treatment decisions.

Animal Bite Assessment & Management

Wound assessment priorities

Work through an animal bite in order:

  • Depth: Did it go through the dermis? Are deeper structures (tendon, bone, vessel) involved?
  • Mechanism: Crush, puncture, tear, or laceration? It changes the risk of infection and how the wound heals.
  • Contamination: Every animal bite is contaminated. Note the species, and whether the animal was provoked.
  • Neurovascular status: Check distal pulses, capillary refill, sensation, and movement (motor function). A serious bite may need urgent vascular assessment.
  • Time elapsed: How long ago was the bite? That affects infection risk and decisions about closing the wound.

Wound care principles

Aggressive irrigation is the most important thing you can do for an animal bite. Use clean water or saline, and use a lot: at least 1-2 liters for a significant bite. Irrigation flushes out the bacteria and debris that would otherwise set up an infection. Do not close a bite wound in the wilderness, with sutures or with adhesive strips. Leave it open to drain and heal from the bottom up (secondary intention). Closing it seals the bacteria in and raises the infection risk.

Dogs & cats

The most common source of bites. Cat bites carry a high infection risk. Those needle-like teeth drive bacteria deep, and roughly one in three cat bites gets infected, so get a cat bite seen early even when the wound looks small. Dog bites carry a lower infection risk but still need thorough irrigation. For the rabies assessment, find out whether the pet's vaccinations are current.

Wild mammals (bear, mountain lion)

Crush and laceration injuries with severe tissue damage, often with major bleeding and deep tissue trauma. Treat it as major trauma until proven otherwise.

Rodents & rabbits

Small puncture wounds with a low rabies risk. Irrigate them clean. Infection risk is low to moderate. Rabies from rodents and rabbits is close to unheard of. Low risk still isn't zero.

Human bites

High infection risk from the bacteria in the mouth (oral flora). They're dirtier than many animal bites. Treat them aggressively: irrigate thoroughly, evacuate, and do not close the wound.

Rabies assessment & management

Rabies is nearly 100% fatal without post-exposure prophylaxis

Once symptoms appear, rabies is virtually always fatal. Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) is highly effective if it's given before symptoms develop.

First step in the field: wash any bite or scratch from a possibly rabid animal right away, thoroughly, with soap and water, for several minutes. Vigorous washing significantly lowers the risk of rabies transmission. It's a specific, proven step, so do it before anything else.

Risk Category Animal Examples
High risk Bats, raccoons, foxes, skunks, coyotes, unvaccinated dogs and cats
Low risk Rodents (squirrels, rats, mice), rabbits and hares (lagomorphs)

Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP)

PEP is a series of rabies vaccines (current versions use 4 doses over 14 days), plus rabies immunoglobulin for high-risk exposures. Start it as soon as possible after exposure, ideally within days. There's no deadline after which PEP stops being indicated. Someone who shows up late, even weeks after exposure, should still get PEP as long as they haven't developed symptoms. If the patient is bitten in the wilderness, evacuate them urgently for medical evaluation and to start PEP. There's no field alternative to hospital-based PEP.

Special consideration: bat exposure

Bats have tiny teeth, and their bites can go unnoticed. If a bat turns up in the room with someone who was asleep, and that person can't rule out direct contact, standard public health guidance is to assume a possible exposure and go ahead with a rabies PEP evaluation. Don't write off a bat exposure as nothing.

Reporting requirements

Public health reporting

Report every significant animal bite to local public health authorities, especially bites from wild animals or unvaccinated domestic ones. Public health officials assess the rabies risk and arrange testing of the animal if they can.

Bear and mountain lion attacks

These are severe trauma. Your first priority is getting the patient out safely, and that starts with making sure the animal is no longer a threat. Scene safety includes the bear. Then treat it as major trauma, with the focus on stopping the bleeding and managing shock. These attacks often mean crush injuries, lacerations to several parts of the body, and possible damage to major vessels. Transport urgently.

Venomous Snakebite Management

North American venomous snakes

Species Characteristics Venom Type & Effects
Pit vipers: rattlesnakes, copperheads, cottonmouths Heat-sensing pits between the eyes and nostrils; triangular head; rattles (rattlesnakes) Hemotoxic: local tissue destruction, swelling that keeps spreading, clotting problems (coagulation abnormalities), bleeding
Coral snake Colorful bands (red, yellow, black); small mouth; no rattles; docile Neurotoxic: nerve symptoms that come on late; may show few local signs at first

Coral snake identification

R
Red touches Yellow, Kill a fellow
R
Red touches Black, Friend of Jack

The rhyme picks out the coral snake, whose red and yellow bands touch each other. Harmless look-alikes such as the kingsnake have red touching black.

Signs of envenomation

Local signs (pit viper)

  • Fang marks (there may be 1-4, depending on the strike angle)
  • Pain at the bite site, right away
  • Swelling that spreads over minutes to hours
  • Mark the edge of the swelling with a pen and write the time, every 15 minutes, so you can track its progress objectively

Systemic signs (pit viper)

  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Weakness and fatigue
  • Numbness and tingling (paresthesias), often around the mouth (perioral)
  • Bleeding or bruising away from the bite (coagulopathy)
  • Low blood pressure, fast heart rate, shock

Systemic signs (coral snake)

  • Delayed onset (it may take hours)
  • Drooping eyelids (ptosis)
  • Double vision (diplopia)
  • Paralysis of the breathing muscles (respiratory paralysis), the most dangerous sign
  • Can happen with few local symptoms at first

Dry bite

About 20% of venomous snakebites inject little or no venom (a "dry bite"). You can't tell which in the field. Every snakebite evacuates now. The 8–12 hours of observation happen at medical care, or on the way there. A dry bite is a diagnosis made in hindsight, not at the trailhead. Your serial swelling marks support that assessment, so document them.

Correct field management for pit viper bites

  • Calm the patient and keep them at rest: movement spreads venom faster.
  • Take off jewelry and tight clothing: the swelling is coming, so get anything constricting off while you still can.
  • Immobilize the limb at heart level: splint or sling it so it can't move. Keep it level with the heart, not elevated, since gravity may increase local swelling.
  • Mark the swelling with a pen and the time, every 15 minutes: the lines work like a river gauge. One reading is a level; a series shows whether it's rising, and that record guides treatment decisions.
  • Urgent evacuation: antivenom is the only definitive treatment, and it has to be given in a hospital. Evacuate immediately.
  • Nothing by mouth: keep them NPO in case they need surgery.
  • Monitor vital signs: reassess XABCDE every 5 minutes, watching for systemic signs.
Do not do any of these
  • Cut an X over the fang marks and suck. It doesn't work, and it adds tissue damage and infection.
  • Use commercial suction or venom-extractor devices (e.g., Sawyer Extractor). They don't remove venom, and they can make tissue damage worse.
  • Apply a tourniquet. It can cause more tissue damage than the venom itself.
  • Apply ice. It causes frostbite and makes tissue damage worse.
  • Use electric shock. There's no evidence it helps, and it causes burns.
  • Put a constriction band or compression wrap on any snakebite. They can increase local tissue damage and the risk of compartment syndrome.

Coral snake bites: same field management

At this course's level, treat every suspected snakebite the same way, pit viper or coral snake: take off jewelry and tight clothing, splint and immobilize the bitten limb at heart level, keep the patient calm and at rest, and evacuate urgently. Do not apply a constriction band or compression wrap of any kind. A "constriction band" isn't the same thing as pressure immobilization bandaging.

Reference note: pressure immobilization bandaging (PIB)

PIB is a specialized elastic-wrap technique that trained providers use for some elapid snakebites, mainly outside North America. Its use for North American coral snakes is controversial, and the evidence behind it is limited. It is not a skill this course teaches, so don't attempt it.

Antivenom

Antivenom is given only in a hospital. Modern pit viper antivenom (CroFab, Anavip) is highly effective if it's given early. Coral snake antivenom is used less often and has to be given carefully, because of possible adverse reactions. Anyone bitten by a possibly venomous species gets evacuated for hospital evaluation and possible antivenom, whatever their early symptoms.

Insect Stings & Spider Bites

Hymenoptera stings: bees, wasps, yellow jackets, hornets, fire ants

Bee stingers

Honeybee stingers are barbed. They stay in the skin and keep injecting venom after the bee has flown off. Get the stinger out fast. Flick it, scrape it, pull it, whatever's quickest. Speed matters; technique doesn't. The venom sac keeps pumping while you hunt for a credit card.

Local reaction

Pain, redness, and swelling at the sting. Treat it with ice, NSAIDs, and a topical antihistamine or corticosteroid cream.

Large local reaction

Swelling that spreads far past the sting, sometimes reaching the joints, like a hand sting that swells the whole arm. This isn't anaphylaxis. It's a localized hypersensitivity reaction. Treat it with an oral antihistamine, and get a medical evaluation if the swelling is severe or still spreading. Watch for anaphylaxis (see Lesson 10).

Fire ants

Fire ant stings leave sterile pustules. Don't pop them, because that lets infection in. Left intact, they'll usually clear up without complications.

Black widow spider envenomation

Identification

A shiny black spider with a distinctive red hourglass on the abdomen. The body's about the size of a pea.

Venom & effects

Black widow venom is neurotoxic (alpha-latrotoxin). It targets the presynaptic nerve terminals and makes them release acetylcholine uncontrollably, which produces the classic symptoms.

Signs & symptoms

  • Pain at the bite, often minimal at first
  • Severe, painful muscle cramps spreading from the bite, within 30 minutes to hours
  • The classic sign is a rigid abdomen as the abdominal muscles cramp, but cramping can hit any muscle group
  • Sweating (diaphoresis), high blood pressure, fast heart rate
  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Pain in the thighs and chest
  • Possible breathing trouble if the respiratory muscles are involved

Field management

Manage the pain with NSAIDs and ice. Evacuation is recommended: hospitals carry black widow antivenom, and it's highly effective. Watch for respiratory distress.

Brown recluse spider envenomation

Identification

A brown spider with a distinctive violin-shaped marking on the front body section (cephalothorax). It has 6 eyes arranged in pairs; most spiders have 8.

Geography matters. Brown recluses live only in the south-central and midwestern United States. A spider that doesn't live in your state is a poor suspect. Outside that range, most suspected "recluse bites" turn out to be something else, commonly a skin infection such as MRSA, or another condition. Even inside recluse territory, confirmed severe necrosis is uncommon.

Venom & effects

Brown recluse venom is cytotoxic and kills tissue (necrosis). As the tissue dies, it leaves the classic "sinking" or "volcano" lesion.

Signs & symptoms

  • A painless bite at first (it may go unnoticed)
  • 2-8 hours: pain starts, redness develops, a blister forms
  • 24-72 hours: a blue-black dead (necrotic) center forms, and the lesion "sinks" as the tissue dies
  • Systemic symptoms are rare but possible: fever, and hemolysis (extremely rare)
  • The lesion can be disfiguring

Field management

Clean the wound, apply ice, and mark the edge of the lesion with a pen to track its progress. Evacuate for medical evaluation. There's no brown recluse antivenom available in the United States. Treatment is supportive and focuses on wound care and preventing infection.

Black widow vs. brown recluse

Feature Black Widow Brown Recluse

Primary effect Muscle cramps, systemic symptoms Tissue necrosis at the bite site
Onset Minutes to hours Hours to days

Tick Removal & Tick-Borne Illness

Removing a tick

Grab the tick with fine-tipped tweezers as close to the skin as you can, and pull steadily upward. Don't twist it, crush it, or use petroleum jelly, alcohol, or heat (burning). Those methods stress the tick and may make it inject more pathogen.

After removal

  • Clean the bite with soap and water
  • Wash your hands
  • Seal the tick in a plastic bag if you can, in case it needs testing later
  • Don't crush the tick with bare hands

Disease transmission timeline

Lyme disease usually needs the tick attached for 36 hours or more to transmit, and the risk climbs steeply after 48 hours. Some other tick-borne diseases transmit faster. Either way, the message is the same. Prompt removal significantly lowers the risk of transmission, and sooner is better.

Tick-borne diseases

Disease Tick Vector Key Clinical Features
Lyme Disease Deer tick (Ixodes) Bull's-eye rash (erythema migrans), flu-like symptoms, arthritis; can involve the central nervous system (CNS)
Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever Dog tick, wood tick Rash that starts at the wrists and ankles and spreads toward the trunk, fever, headache; can be fatal if untreated
Ehrlichiosis Lone Star tick Fever, headache, muscle aches (myalgia), sometimes a rash, low platelets (thrombocytopenia)
Anaplasmosis Black-legged (deer) tick Similar to ehrlichiosis: fever, headache, muscle aches, abnormal lab results

Marine Envenomation

Jellyfish stings

Jellyfish stinging cells (nematocysts) fire when they're triggered and inject venom. Treat it this way:

  • Rinse with salt water, not fresh water, which can make the nematocysts fire
  • Don't use urine. It doesn't help, and it can make more nematocysts fire.
  • Remove visible tentacles with tweezers, not bare hands
  • Vinegar helps deactivate box jellyfish stings, but it can make some other species' stings worse. Use it only when you know a box jellyfish did it.
  • If you don't know the species: rinse with seawater, remove the tentacles with tweezers, then use hot water immersion (45°C / 113°F) for 20-45 minutes for the pain

Stingray injury

A stingray spine makes a puncture wound and injects venom, usually into the foot or ankle. The pain is severe.

  • Immerse the limb in hot water (45°C / 113°F) for 30-90 minutes. Early on, that matters more than getting the spine out.
  • Irrigate the wound
  • Remove the barb if you can reach it without causing more injury
  • Evacuate for wound care and an evaluation for deeper injury

Sea urchin spines

Sea urchin spines embed in the skin and can break off. They can be toxic and irritating.

  • Remove the spines you can reach with tweezers
  • Use hot water immersion (45°C / 113°F)
  • Evacuate if multiple spines are embedded or there's a risk of infection

Scenario-Based Learning

Scenario 1: Rattlesnake on the Trail

Setting: A hiker 5 miles from the trailhead says a snake bit them on the ankle. They think it was a rattlesnake. You get there 10 minutes after the bite. The ankle is swollen and bruised, with two fang marks. The patient is anxious and nauseated.

Think it through:

  • What are your first assessment priorities?
  • What will you do for them in the field?
  • What are you not going to do?
  • What's your evacuation plan?
Working it through:
  • Primary assessment: Make sure the snake isn't still around (scene safety). Run XABCDE. Is the circulation unstable (hemodynamic instability)? Are they in respiratory distress? Any signs of systemic envenomation, such as nausea, vomiting, or low blood pressure?
  • Secondary assessment: Note the time of the bite. Look at the site. Two fang marks suggest a pit viper. It could be a dry bite, but assume envenomation. Mark the baseline swelling with a pen and write down the time.
  • Field interventions: Carefully remove the boot and sock. Take off any jewelry, rings included. Immobilize the limb, with the ankle at about heart level, not elevated. Nothing by mouth (NPO).
  • What not to do: No cutting and sucking. No tourniquet or constriction band. No ice. Don't try to kill or capture the snake. One patient is plenty.
  • Evacuation: 5 miles from the trailhead at 30 minutes a mile (a slow pace with an immobilized patient) is about 2.5 hours on foot. This needs an urgent evacuation. If you have a satellite communicator, activate it now for rescue dispatch. Call for a helicopter if one's available and the weather allows. Don't delay the evacuation.
  • Monitoring: Check vitals every 5 minutes. Re-mark the swelling every 15 minutes. Watch for signs of systemic envenomation. Keep the patient at rest, and reassure them. Anxiety drives up the sympathetic response, and that can make symptoms worse.
  • Definitive care: Every pit viper bite needs hospital evaluation for possible antivenom, whatever the symptoms. Even a dry bite warrants observation.
Scenario 2: Bat in the Cabin

Setting: You're on camp staff. In the morning, a cabin of 8 campers (ages 12-15) tells you they woke up and found a bat had been in the cabin with them overnight. Nobody reports a bite, but several of them were sleeping soundly. The bat has flown off.

Think it through:

  • Is this a rabies exposure?
  • What do you need to do?
The call, and what you do:
  • Yes, this is a rabies exposure. Bats have tiny teeth and can bite during sleep without anyone noticing. Standard public health guidance: if a bat is found in a room with a sleeping person and direct contact can't be ruled out, assume a possible exposure.
  • Don't dismiss it. "No visible bite" does not mean "no exposure."
  • Right now: Call the local health department and the medical director immediately. All 8 campers need evaluation for post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP).
  • Notification: Notify every camper's parents or guardians right away. Write down when the bat was found, which campers were in the cabin, and any symptoms.
  • The bat: Don't try to catch or kill it. That's one more exposure risk. If it's already contained, contact public health for safe handling and possible testing.
  • Next steps: Arrange urgent medical evaluation for every camper. PEP has to start as soon as possible after exposure. Today's vaccines are much safer than the ones used in the past.

Lesson Summary

Key takeaways

  • Irrigate every animal bite aggressively and leave it open to drain. Don't close it in the wilderness.
  • A bat in the room with a sleeping person means you assume exposure. That needs a PEP evaluation, even with no visible bite.
  • Pit viper bites: immobilize, mark the swelling, and no tourniquet, ice, or constriction. Evacuate for antivenom.
  • Coral snake bites are neurotoxic. They may show few local signs and still cause respiratory failure. Manage them like any snakebite: immobilize at heart level, no bands or wraps, evacuate urgently.
  • Get bee stingers out fast. Speed matters more than method.
  • Black widow venom causes muscle cramps and systemic symptoms. Brown recluse venom causes local tissue necrosis.
  • Pull ticks out promptly with fine tweezers and steady upward traction. Lyme usually needs 36+ hours of attachment, but some diseases transmit faster. The sooner it's out, the lower the risk.
  • Marine envenomation: salt water for jellyfish, hot water immersion for stingray and sea urchin.

Glossary of terms

Envenomation
An animal (snake, spider, insect) injecting venom into tissue.
Hemotoxin
Venom that acts on the blood cells and circulatory system, destroying tissue locally and disrupting clotting. Typical of pit vipers.
Neurotoxin
Venom that acts on the nervous system and can cause paralysis. Typical of coral snakes and some spiders.
Antivenom
Antibodies against a specific venom, given intravenously to counteract envenomation. Used for pit vipers, black widows, and other serious bites.
Rabies
A viral infection spread through saliva. Nearly always fatal once symptoms appear, and preventable with post-exposure prophylaxis.
Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP)
A series of vaccines and immunoglobulin given after a possible rabies exposure to prevent infection.
Dry Bite
A venomous snakebite that injects little or no venom. It happens in about 20% of pit viper bites and is diagnosed in hindsight, after 8–12 hours of observation at medical care.
Black Widow Spider
A venomous spider with a distinctive red hourglass marking. Its venom is neurotoxic and causes muscle cramps and systemic symptoms.
Brown Recluse Spider
A venomous spider with a violin-shaped marking. Its venom is cytotoxic and causes local tissue necrosis in a "sinking lesion" pattern.
Tick-Borne Illness
Infections spread by infected ticks, including Lyme disease, Rocky Mountain spotted fever, ehrlichiosis, and anaplasmosis.
Necrotizing
Causing tissue death. Characteristic of brown recluse venom.
Pressure Immobilization
A specialized elastic-bandage technique that trained providers use for some elapid snakebites to slow the spread of venom through the lymphatics. It's controversial, with limited evidence for North American coral snakes. It's here for awareness only; this course doesn't teach it.
