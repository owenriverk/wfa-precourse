Overview: Heat Emergencies in Wilderness

Heat illness is a spectrum, from cramps to a killer. The skill is knowing where your patient sits on it.

Cool now. Transport after. Heat stroke (core temp over 40°C with altered mental status) is an immediate threat to life. It needs aggressive cooling right away, not a ride out first.

Why heat illness matters in the wilderness

  • Exertion in heat: hiking, climbing, and mountaineering make metabolic heat. Add a hot day and core temperature climbs fast.
  • Limited access to cooling: out in remote country, there may be no cool water.
  • Dehydration: water can run short on a trip, and dehydration cuts how much heat you shed by sweating.
  • Delayed evacuation: it can be hours before you reach a hospital, so the cooling has to happen in the field.

Key points

Heat stroke is a core temp over 40°C (104°F) with altered mental status. It's a medical emergency.
For heat stroke, cool first and transport second.
Cold water immersion is the most effective way to cool someone in the field.
Hyponatremia (too much water) can look like heat exhaustion, but the treatment is different.

Thermoregulation: How Body Maintains Temperature

Heat production

  • Basal metabolism: ordinary cell work makes heat
  • Muscle activity (exercise): the biggest heat source in someone who's active
  • Shivering: involuntary muscle contractions that make heat in the cold

How the body loses heat

  • Radiation: the body gives off infrared heat to its surroundings. It's the largest route of heat loss at rest, with no exertion.
  • Conduction: direct contact. Heat moves into a cooler surface, like the ground, water, or a cold object.
  • Convection: moving air or water carries heat away. Wind speeds it up, and water does it much better than air.
  • Evaporation: sweat evaporating off the skin. It's the most effective route during exercise. High humidity slows evaporation, which limits cooling.
  • Respiration: a minor route. Exhaled air carries some heat out.

Thermoregulation center

  • Hypothalamus: works like a thermostat. It senses changes in core temperature.
  • Responses to high temperature:
    • Cutaneous vasodilation: vessels near the skin open up, so more heat leaves by radiation and conduction
    • Sweating: puts moisture on the skin for evaporative cooling (the most effective route in heat and exercise)

Risk factors for heat illness

  • Environmental: high temperature, high humidity (which slows sweat evaporation), direct sun, no breeze
  • Exertion: hard activity makes metabolic heat
  • Dehydration: impairs sweating, so there's less fluid to evaporate
  • Medications:
    • Anticholinergics (the kind that cause dry mouth): reduce sweating
    • Diuretics (water pills): cause fluid loss
    • Antipsychotics: interfere with heat regulation
    • Beta-blockers: reduce exercise tolerance
  • Alcohol: impairs judgment, and causes vasodilation (more heat loss at rest) and dehydration
  • Obesity: extra insulation, and less surface area for heat loss relative to body mass
  • Age extremes: the very young and the elderly regulate heat less well
  • Prior heat illness: raises the risk of it happening again, and may permanently lower heat tolerance

Heat Illness Spectrum: Mild to Severe

Heat cramps

What: painful, involuntary muscle spasms, usually in the legs and abdomen

  • When: during or right after exercise in the heat
  • Cause: lost salt (sodium) and fluid, plus tired muscles
  • Signs: muscle pain and spasms in the legs or abdomen. The patient is usually alert and sweating, with a normal temperature.
  • Treatment:
    • Stop the activity and rest somewhere cool
    • Oral electrolyte drink (sports drink, salty broth). They need salt and fluid.
    • Gently stretch the cramping muscles
    • Usually settles on its own in 15 to 20 minutes

Heat syncope (fainting)

  • What: a brief loss of consciousness from reduced blood flow to the brain
  • Mechanism: vasodilation lets blood pool in the arms and legs, and blood pressure drops
  • When: during or shortly after standing in the heat
  • Treatment:
    • Lay them flat on their back (supine)
    • Move them somewhere cool
    • Oral fluids if they're conscious
    • Usually benign. They recover quickly.

Heat exhaustion

What: the whole body responding to heat. It's struggling, but it's still working. Core temp is usually under 40°C.

  • Signs:
    • Heavy sweating (clothes may be soaked through)
    • Weakness, dizziness, headache
    • Nausea or vomiting
    • Fast pulse (tachycardia)
    • Cool, pale, clammy skin. That's from evaporating sweat and relative hypovolemia, not vasoconstriction. In the heat, the skin's vessels are actually dilated.
  • Treatment:
    • Move them to cool shade or air conditioning
    • Take off extra clothing
    • Cool them actively: cool or cold water, fanning, ice packs if you have them
    • Oral rehydration with cool water or an electrolyte drink, if they can drink
    • Rest
    • Keep watching. They should improve within 30 minutes.
    • If they don't improve, or they get worse, evacuate. It may be heat stroke.

Heat stroke: the emergency

Definition: a core temperature over 40°C (104°F) with altered mental status. It's a medical emergency.

  • Two types:
    • Classic heat stroke: elderly, sedentary people after long exposure to a hot environment. Often hot, dry skin, because the sweating mechanism has failed.
    • Exertional heat stroke: young, healthy athletes during hard exercise. They may still be sweating despite a high core temp.
  • Signs of heat stroke:
    • Core temp over 40°C (104°F), if measured
    • Altered mental status: confusion, combativeness, irrational behavior, unconsciousness
    • Hot skin, dry or wet depending on the type
    • Fast heart rate and fast breathing (tachycardia, tachypnea)
    • Possible seizures
  • Why it's an emergency: a high core temp damages the brain, heart, kidneys, and liver. Organ failure and death can come fast.

Heat Stroke: Emergency Management

Cool first, transport second. Aggressive, immediate cooling is the priority in heat stroke, and every minute counts. The helicopter won't be bringing a cold tub.

Recognition

  • Heat exposure + altered mental status + hot skin = heat stroke until proven otherwise
  • They may be unconscious (severe) or just confused or combative (milder, but still heat stroke)
  • If the picture fits, don't wait for a thermometer to confirm it

Cooling methods, ranked by effectiveness

1. Cold water immersion (most effective)

  • Use a tub, a stream, or a large container of cool or cold water
  • Immerse them up to the neck, with the head out of the water so you can manage the airway
  • It can drop core temp 0.1 to 0.2°C per minute
  • Watch for seizures and vomiting
  • The only catch: it's not always available in the wilderness. If you're near a river or lake, use it.

2. Ice packs to high blood flow areas

  • Put them on the neck, armpits (axillae), and groin, where large blood vessels run close to the skin
  • Ice straight on skin can cause frostbite, so wrap it in cloth
  • Effective, but slower than immersion

3. Evaporative cooling

  • Wet their skin with cool water
  • Fan them, or wave a cloth, to move air over the skin (convection)
  • As the water evaporates, it carries heat away
  • Effective, but slower than immersion

Cooling endpoint

  • Stop cooling when core temp reaches 39°C (102.2°F)
  • Keep cooling past that and you risk overshoot: the temperature drops too far and they become hypothermic
  • No thermometer? Don't wait for one. Cool until mental status clearly improves, shivering starts, or both.

Supportive care during cooling

  • Airway: position them to keep the airway safe, and watch their breathing
  • Recovery position: if they're unconscious and there's no spine concern, roll them onto their side so vomit drains out instead of being inhaled (aspiration)
  • Watch for complications:
    • Seizures: they can happen with heat stroke. Don't restrain them; protect them from injury.
    • Vomiting: aspiration risk. Position them so it drains.
  • Do not give oral fluids: they may not be able to swallow safely

Evacuation

  • Every heat stroke is an urgent evacuation, even if they improve with cooling
  • Complications can show up hours after cooling
  • They need hospital evaluation and monitoring
  • Call for a helicopter if one's available. They need advanced care.

Hyponatremia: Water Intoxication

What is hyponatremia?

  • Definition: serum sodium under 135 mEq/L (normal is about 140)
  • Cause: drinking too much plain water without replacing sodium, especially during long endurance efforts
  • Common in: marathon and ultramarathon runners, and on very long hiking days

How it happens

  • An athlete or hiker drinks large amounts of plain water, with no electrolytes
  • Intake outpaces both sweat loss and urine output
  • Blood sodium gets diluted
  • Water moves into cells (an osmotic effect), and the brain swells (cerebral edema)

Signs and symptoms

  • Nausea, headache, dizziness
  • Confusion, disorientation
  • Seizures in severe cases
  • Looks like heat exhaustion: weakness, confusion, and it seems like they've overheated
Ask what they've been drinking. The hyponatremia patient has drunk a lot of plain water, far more than normal, and their urine is clear. A heat exhaustion patient may have dark urine from dehydration.

Field treatment

  • Stop the water: do not give more plain water
  • Restrict fluid: limit total fluids
  • Sodium, for alert patients only: only if they're fully alert, can swallow, and can protect their own airway, encourage salty food (nuts, broth, pretzels, beef jerky). Any confusion, vomiting, or seizure means nothing by mouth and urgent evacuation.
  • Evacuation: symptomatic hyponatremia needs hospital evaluation. Hospital care may include 3% hypertonic saline. That's a hospital treatment, not a field one.
Plain water can kill this patient. Mistake hyponatremia for heat exhaustion, hand over more water, and it can be fatal. The water makes hyponatremia worse.

Prevention

  • Drink to thirst, not beyond. Avoiding overdrinking is the main way to prevent exercise-associated hyponatremia. Sports drinks are hypotonic, and they do not reliably prevent it if people drink too much of them.
  • Over several hours of exertion, salty snacks and electrolyte drinks help replace the sodium lost in sweat. They don't protect against drinking too much fluid.

Prevention of Heat Illness

Heat acclimatization

  • What: the body adapting to heat exposure over time
  • Timeline: 10 to 14 days of gradually increasing activity in the heat
  • What changes:
    • Sweating starts earlier, and there's more of it
    • Less sodium in sweat (the body holds on to its salt)
    • More plasma volume (more blood, better cooling)
    • Better cardiac output
    • Lower core temperature during exercise
  • Plan for it: acclimatized people handle heat much better. If you're heading somewhere hot, build up your heat exposure gradually.

WBGT: wet bulb globe temperature

  • What: the most accurate index of heat stress. It accounts for temperature, humidity, radiant heat, and wind.
  • Why it beats air temperature alone: humidity has a big effect on how well sweat can cool you
  • Uses:
    • Sports and military programs set activity limits by WBGT thresholds
    • A typical threshold: suspend strenuous activity when WBGT is over 32°C (90°F)
    • Work-rest ratios: at high WBGT, build in frequent breaks and hydration

Hydration guidelines

  • Drink to thirst, not beyond: the primary rule. Not overdrinking is what prevents hyponatremia.
  • Pre-hydration: start the activity well hydrated
  • For exertion over several hours: salty snacks and electrolyte drinks help replace sodium lost in sweat, but they do not protect against overdrinking
  • Avoid overhydrating: drinking more water than you need raises the risk of hyponatremia

Clothing and environment

  • Clothing: light colors (they reflect heat), a loose fit (so air moves), moisture-wicking fabric
  • Sun protection: a hat and sunscreen to cut radiant heat absorption
  • Activity timing: avoid peak sun hours (10am to 4pm) when you can. The miles are just as long at dawn, and a lot cooler.
  • Buddy system: watch your partners for early signs of heat illness. People often can't recognize their own symptoms.

Case Scenarios

Scenario 1: Runner collapses at a wilderness marathon finish

Setting: A runner finishes a wilderness marathon (26 miles) on a hot day and collapses at the line, confused and agitated. Her skin is hot and flushed. Bystanders say she's "acting weird" and seems disoriented. There's no tap or tub, but there's a creek nearby.

Recognition: Heat exposure + altered mental status + hot skin = heat stroke.

What you do now:

  • Get her to the creek immediately
  • Immerse her in the cool water up to the neck
  • Start cooling now. Don't wait for the evacuation.
  • Watch for seizures and vomiting
  • If you have a thermometer, check her core temp (it's likely over 40°C)

Keep cooling until: her core temp reaches 39°C or, with no thermometer, her mental status clearly improves, she starts shivering, or both.

After cooling: Evacuate urgently for hospital evaluation. Complications can show up hours later.

Scenario 2: Confused hiker who drank too much water

Setting: After a 12-mile hike on a warm day, someone in the group gets confused and complains of a headache. When you ask, they say they drank about 8 liters of water, way more than usual. Their urine is clear. No fever. Their skin is cool and moist, not hot.

Recognition: Lots of plain water + confusion + clear urine = hyponatremia, not heat exhaustion.

Put the water bottle down. This isn't heat exhaustion, so do not give more water.

Field treatment:

  • Stop the water intake immediately
  • They're confused, so nothing by mouth: no salty snacks, no fluids. The airway isn't safe.
  • Protect the airway, and if they vomit, position them so it drains
  • Rest them somewhere cool and watch them closely

Evacuation: Symptomatic hyponatremia needs urgent hospital evaluation. Hospital care may include 3% hypertonic saline.

Next time: Prevent hyponatremia by drinking to thirst, not beyond. Over several hours of exertion, salty snacks and electrolyte drinks help replace sodium, but they don't protect against overdrinking. And salty food or fluid is only for a fully alert patient who can swallow safely.

Glossary

Thermoregulation
The body's ability to hold a steady core temperature by balancing heat production and heat loss
Heat Cramps
Painful muscle spasms during or after exertion in the heat, caused by salt and fluid loss
Heat Syncope
Fainting from vasodilation and blood pooling during or after standing in the heat
Heat Exhaustion
The whole-body response to heat stress (sweating, weakness, nausea, fast pulse) without altered mental status
Heat Stroke
Core temperature over 40°C with altered mental status. A medical emergency that needs immediate cooling.
Hyponatremia
Low serum sodium from drinking too much plain water without replacing electrolytes
WBGT
Wet bulb globe temperature. The most accurate heat stress index, accounting for temperature, humidity, radiant heat, and wind.
Evaporative Cooling
Heat loss as sweat or applied water evaporates. Most effective during exercise.
Heat Acclimatization
The body adapting to heat over 10 to 14 days of exposure. It improves sweating, plasma volume, and heat tolerance.
Exertional Heat Illness
Heat illness during exercise, especially in young athletes. They may keep sweating even at a high core temperature.
