Head, Neck & Spine Injuries

Spine assessment, TBI, concussion, helmet removal, logroll.

Overview

Learning Objectives

  • Know the basic anatomy of the spine and how spine injuries happen
  • Recognize when spine precautions are needed, and know the reference criteria higher-trained rescuers use to discontinue them
  • Do manual in-line stabilization and a proper logroll
  • Apply spinal motion restriction (SMR) with generous padding and improvised materials
  • Decide when a helmet stays on and when it comes off, and do a two-rescuer helmet removal without traction
  • Recognize and manage traumatic brain injury (TBI), including concussion
  • Spot the signs of increased intracranial pressure (ICP) and of a basilar skull fracture
  • Understand GCS scoring and when a TBI patient needs to evacuate

A spine injury is the one you can make permanently worse with your own hands. Move slow, think first. A simple fall can injure the spinal cord and leave someone permanently paralyzed. A bump on the head can cause a traumatic brain injury, with effects that are subtle and still serious. At this level of training the rule is simple: if the mechanism or the symptoms suggest a possible spine injury, keep the patient still and evacuate. Discontinuing spine precautions is a decision for higher-trained rescuers or medical direction.

Two jobs, then. With a possible spine injury, you restrict motion. With a head injury, you watch for decline, whether it's a concussion or pressure building inside the skull.

When in doubt, keep it still. If a spine injury is possible, immobilize and stabilize until it's proven otherwise. Spinal cord damage is irreversible, so erring conservative is justified.

Spinal Anatomy & Injury Mechanisms

Spinal column regions

Region Vertebrae Location Injury Patterns
Cervical (C) C1-C7 Neck; the most mobile and least protected High-risk region; injuries are often catastrophic (quadriplegia)
Thoracic (T) T1-T12 Mid-back; protected by the rib cage Often comes with significant trauma; spinal cord injury is common
Lumbar (L) L1-L5 Lower back; large, weight-bearing vertebrae (the thoracic spine, braced by the rib cage, is the most rigid region) Fractures are common. Below about L1-2 the canal holds nerve roots (the cauda equina) instead of spinal cord, and cauda equina syndrome is a surgical emergency in its own right.
Sacral (S) S1-S5 (fused) Lowest spine; fused to the pelvis Part of the pelvis; fractures usually take extreme trauma

Spinal cord anatomy

The spinal cord is a bundle of nerves running through the vertebral column from the brainstem to about L1. Nerve roots branch off at each level to supply the body. Damage the cord at any level, and function below that level is disrupted:

  • Cervical injury (C1-C4): can be fatal, because C3-C5 control breathing. Causes quadriplegia (paralysis of all four limbs).
  • Cervical injury (C5-C8): quadriplegia, with variable arm and hand function
  • Thoracic injury: paraplegia (paralysis of the legs)
  • Lumbar injury: cauda equina syndrome (variable leg paralysis, bowel and bladder dysfunction), a surgical emergency

Mechanisms of spinal injury

Axial load (compression): Force driven along the length of the spine. Examples: a diver hitting the bottom, a fall onto the buttocks with the legs straight, a fall from height landing on the feet. It causes vertebral compression fractures.

Hyperflexion: The spine bent too far forward. Examples: a head-on collision, being hit from behind. It can fracture vertebrae and rupture ligaments.

Hyperextension: The spine bent too far backward. Examples: a rear-end collision, a blow to the forehead. It can cause fractures and spinal cord damage.

Rotation/distraction: Twisting or pulling forces. Examples: a rollover, a body being twisted. These are high-energy injuries, and they often come with fracture-dislocations and severe cord injury.

For reference: NEXUS criteria

Hospital providers use the NEXUS criteria to decide who needs C-spine imaging. NEXUS can't rule out an injury in a patient with any of these:

  • Midline cervical spine tenderness
  • A focal neurological deficit (weakness, numbness, paralysis)
  • An altered level of alertness
  • Intoxication
  • A painful distracting injury

At the WFA level, a concerning mechanism (a significant fall, a high-speed impact, an axial load) or any of the findings above is reason to keep the patient still and evacuate.

Wilderness Spine Assessment (WSA)

Who decides to discontinue spine precautions?

At the Wilderness First Aid level, you don't "clear" the spine. If the mechanism or the symptoms suggest a possible spine injury, keep the patient still and evacuate. The decision to discontinue spine precautions belongs to rescuers with higher-level training, or to medical direction.

For reference: the Wilderness Spine Assessment criteria, used by higher-trained rescuers. Spine precautions can be discontinued only when all of these are true:
  • A reliable patient: calm, cooperative, sober, and alert
  • No spine pain and no midline tenderness when you press on it
  • Normal motor and sensory function in all four limbs
  • No significant distracting injury (one painful enough to mask spine symptoms)
If any one of them isn't met, or the assessment can't be completed, precautions stay on and the patient is evacuated.

Manual in-line stabilization

If a spine injury is possible, stabilize the head and neck:

  1. Hold the patient's head with a hand on each side, keeping it neutral: not bent forward, bent back, or turned (flexed, extended, or rotated)
  2. Support the head in neutral alignment without pulling. Never apply traction to a suspected spine injury; it can make an unstable injury worse. Stop any repositioning if you meet resistance, the pain increases, or the patient reports new tingling or numbness.
  3. Don't let the head move in any direction
  4. Keep holding until the patient is fully immobilized on a stretcher

Logroll technique

To move a patient with a suspected spine injury from the ground to a stretcher, logroll them to keep the spine straight:

What a logroll takes:
  • At least 3 rescuers: one on the head and neck, one on the torso, one on the legs
  • 4 or more is ideal
  • One rescuer directs the move, usually the one holding the head. Nobody rolls until that person calls it.
  • Roll the patient as one unit. Don't let the spine bend or twist.
  • Lay them on the stretcher on their back (supine)

Spinal motion restriction (SMR)

The goal is spinal motion restriction (SMR): keep the patient still, pad generously under and around them, and stabilize the head with rolled blankets, clothing, or foam on both sides. What counts is improvised padding that actually limits motion. On the long evacuations typical of the wilderness, comfort and generous padding matter too.

For reference: cervical collars. EMS may apply a rigid cervical collar, but rigid collars are de-emphasized now. They can cause pressure injuries, and they don't immobilize the neck well. A collar alone is never enough. Manual stabilization and padding that limits motion matter more.

Helmet Removal

On a motorcycle or rafting trip, a lot of head and spine injuries come wearing a helmet. A snug helmet isn't the enemy. It splints the head much the way your hands do during manual stabilization. So skip "do helmets come off?" and ask the real question: does this helmet, on this patient, right now, help or hurt? Mostly, that's an airway decision.

Leave it on or take it off?

Leave the helmet on when... Take the helmet off when...
The patient is breathing adequately You can't assess or manage the airway through it
The airway is accessible and you can monitor it The patient is vomiting or needs rescue breathing or CPR
The helmet fits snugly and is splinting the head for you Breathing is compromised, or the helmet is loose (a loose helmet stabilizes nothing)
Taking it off would cause more movement than leaving it on The helmet keeps you from holding the head in neutral alignment
Full-face vs. open-face. A full-face motorcycle helmet usually has to come off for any airway problem. You can't manage an airway through a chin bar. An open-face or whitewater helmet leaves the face exposed, so a patient who's breathing can often keep it on while you stabilize and monitor.

Preparation first

Before anyone touches the helmet itself, set up:

  1. Tell a conscious patient exactly what you're about to do
  2. Raise the visor all the way
  3. Take off the patient's glasses or goggles
  4. Unbuckle the chin strap, or cut it if the buckle fights you
  5. Remove the cheek pads if the design allows. Many full-face helmets have pull-out pads, and taking them out makes room and cuts down on movement.

Two-rescuer removal technique

  1. Rescuer 1 stabilizes from above: kneeling at the top of the head, hands on both sides of the helmet, fingertips on the patient's jaw.
  2. Rescuer 2 takes over stabilization from below: kneeling at the patient's side, one hand cupping the jaw (mandible), the other behind the back of the skull (occiput). Rescuer 2 now owns the head and says so out loud: "I have stabilization."
  3. Rescuer 1 removes the helmet: spread the sides outward to clear the ears, then rotate it. Tilt it back slightly so the chin bar clears the nose, then forward to clear the back of the skull. Slide it off slowly and straight. Never jerk it.
  4. Rescuer 2 keeps neutral support the whole time and calls out immediately if anything changes: resistance, pain, or new tingling or numbness.
  5. Rescuer 1 takes back manual in-line stabilization as soon as the helmet is off.
  6. Pad under the occiput: a full-face helmet held the head up, so once it's off, the head will drop toward the ground unless there's padding under it. Slide folded clothing under the back of the skull to bring it back to neutral alignment.
No traction, ever. At no point in a helmet removal does anyone pull on the head or neck. Support the head in neutral without pulling. If you meet resistance, the pain increases, or the patient reports new tingling or numbness, stop and reassess before you go on.

Solo rescuer

Take a helmet off alone only if the airway needs it right now and no help is available. One person can't stabilize the head and work the helmet at the same time, so a solo removal always costs more movement. If the patient is breathing, stabilize the head, monitor, and wait for a second rescuer.

Riders: practice this with your group before the trip. The first time you take off a full-face helmet shouldn't be on an injured friend.

Same principle for gear: if a bike, pack, or PFD is pinning the patient and you suspect a spine injury, move the obstacle, not the patient.

Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI)

What a concussion is

A concussion is a type of TBI: a blow to the head or a violent head movement disrupts normal brain function. There may or may not be a loss of consciousness. Recovery is a gradual return of function.

Concussion signs and symptoms

  • Headache (the most common)
  • Confusion or disorientation ("What's my name?" "Where am I?")
  • Dizziness or vertigo
  • Nausea or vomiting
  • Trouble concentrating, or memory problems
  • Sensitivity to light or noise
  • Loss of consciousness (it varies, and it isn't required for the diagnosis)
  • Amnesia for events before or after the injury

Return-to-play protocol

Care after a concussion starts with 24-48 hours of relative rest. After that comes a gradual, symptom-limited return to activity, where activity shouldn't make symptoms more than mildly worse:

Stage Activity Level Duration
1 Relative rest (limit screens and exertion; normal daily activities as tolerated) 24-48 hours
2 Light aerobic activity (walking, stationary bike) As tolerated, 24+ hours at stage
3 Sport-specific exercise As tolerated, 24+ hours at stage
4 Non-contact training As tolerated, 24+ hours at stage
5 Full-contact practice As tolerated, 24+ hours at stage
6 Return to competition Full return

The rule at every stage: activity shouldn't make symptoms more than mildly worse. If symptoms worsen, drop back to the previous stage. No return to sport or other high-risk activities until a medical professional gives the go-ahead.

Overnight monitoring in the backcountry

Let them sleep. Keep checking on them.
  • Sleep is allowed after a concussion, and it helps. Don't force the patient to stay awake. The all-night vigil is campfire folklore.
  • Watch for deterioration: every few hours during the first night, check that they can be roused (they wake and respond appropriately)
  • Never leave a concussed person alone in the backcountry
  • A worsening headache, repeated vomiting, or declining alertness means an emergent evacuation

Postconcussion syndrome

In some patients, symptoms hang on for weeks to months after a concussion. They can include persistent headache, trouble thinking (cognitive dysfunction), and mood changes. Recovery takes longer, and a specialist evaluation may help.

Cushing's triad: a sign of rising intracranial pressure

Cushing's triad is a late sign of increased ICP:
  • High blood pressure (hypertension): a progressive, severe rise, with a widening pulse pressure
  • Slow heart rate (bradycardia): under 60 bpm despite shock from the injury
  • Irregular breathing: an abnormal pattern (Cheyne-Stokes, gasping)
Cushing's triad means an emergent situation. Brain herniation may be imminent.

Basilar skull fracture signs

A fracture at the base of the skull can be subtle on exam, and it's serious. Look for:

Sign Finding Significance
Battle's Sign Bruising behind the ear (over the mastoid bone) Suggests a basilar skull fracture
Raccoon Eyes Bruising around both eyes (with no direct trauma to the eyes) Suggests a basilar fracture of the anterior fossa (the front of the skull base)
CSF Rhinorrhea Clear fluid draining from the nose CSF leaking through a fracture of the cribriform plate
CSF Otorrhea Clear fluid draining from the ear CSF leaking through a temporal bone fracture
Hemotympanum Blood behind the eardrum (tympanum) Suggests a basilar fracture

Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)

GCS measures level of consciousness from three things: eye opening, verbal response, and motor response. Scores run from 3 to 15. The lower the score, the more severe the injury.

Reading the GCS:
  • 13-15: mild TBI
  • 9-12: moderate TBI
  • 3-8: severe TBI (they can't protect their own airway)

Signs a TBI patient is getting worse

Any of these means immediate evacuation:
  • Unequal pupils (anisocoria), which suggest brain herniation
  • A worsening headache or mental status
  • Vomiting, especially projectile
  • Loss of consciousness
  • Seizures
  • Cushing's triad (hypertension, bradycardia, irregular respirations)

Field Management of Head & Spine Injuries

Positioning

Suspected spine injury: Keep them on their back (supine) with the head neutral. If they vomit, logroll them to their side, keeping the spine aligned.

Conscious head injury with no suspected spine injury: Sit them upright or semi-upright to reduce intracranial pressure and prevent aspiration.

Airway management priority

Keeping the airway open is the absolute priority. If the patient is unconscious or can't protect their airway:

  • If there's no suspected spine injury, put them in the recovery position (on their side, lateral recumbent) with the head tilted back to keep the airway open
  • If a spine injury is possible, keep them on their back with the head neutral, and be ready for aggressive airway management
  • If they vomit with a suspected spine injury, logroll them as a unit to their side to protect the airway

Vomiting in a spine patient

A vomiting patient with a suspected spine injury is a hard problem in the wilderness, because they can aspirate. The fix:

  1. Keep them on their back with the head neutral as much as you can
  2. If they vomit, logroll them as a unit onto their left side. That keeps the spine aligned.
  3. Suction the airway if you have suction
  4. Roll them back onto their back once the vomiting clears

When to move a spine-injured patient

Move them right away for:

  • An environmental danger (fire, rockfall, water)
  • Airway compromise that needs repositioning
  • Shock that needs treatment

Otherwise: Keep movement to a minimum. Keep them still until they're fully immobilized on a stretcher.

Evacuation urgency

Emergent evacuation:
  • Any unconscious patient
  • A declining level of consciousness
  • Any neurological deficit (weakness, paralysis, loss of sensation)
  • An unreliable exam (altered mental status, intoxication, a severe distracting injury)
  • Signs of rising ICP (Cushing's triad, unequal pupils, vomiting)
  • A suspected basilar skull fracture
Urgent evacuation:
  • A concussion with any symptoms
  • Mild TBI (GCS 13-15) with a reassuring exam
  • A suspected spine injury with a normal, reliable exam (no deficits; a calm, cooperative, sober, alert patient)

Documentation

Document a TBI patient carefully. Nobody's memory survives a long carry intact.

  • Mechanism of injury
  • Level of consciousness on arrival and throughout the evacuation
  • GCS score
  • Pupils: size, equal or unequal, reactive or not
  • Any vomiting or seizures
  • Vital signs and their trends
  • Any improvement or deterioration

Clinical Scenarios

Scenario 1: Possible Cervical Spine Injury
A 40-year-old rock climber loses control rappelling down a steep descent and lands hard on his upper back and neck. He's conscious, alert, and asking questions. He has mild neck pain but no numbness or weakness. Exam: no midline tenderness and normal strength and sensation in all four limbs. The mechanism was significant (a fall from height).
What's going on: A significant mechanism (a fall from height onto the upper back and neck), plus neck pain. At the WFA level, that means spine precautions and evacuation.

What you do:
  • Keep spine precautions in place. At the WFA level, the decision to discontinue them belongs to higher-trained rescuers or medical direction.
  • Start manual in-line stabilization immediately (support the head in neutral, no pulling).
  • Keep him on his back with the head neutral, and stabilize the head with rolled blankets, clothing, or padding on both sides (spinal motion restriction).
  • Recheck his neuro function often. Any change in strength, sensation, or symptoms?
  • Urgent evacuation for imaging (X-ray or CT) to rule out a fracture.
  • Don't let him walk out or self-rescue.
Precautions stay on. WFA providers keep precautions in place and evacuate. For reference: a rescuer with higher-level training could formally assess him with the wilderness spine assessment criteria. He's calm, alert, and sober, but his neck pain alone would keep precautions in place.
Scenario 2: Traumatic Brain Injury with Deterioration
A 32-year-old hiker is hit on the head by a falling tree branch. At first he's dazed and confused, with a headache and no vomiting. You start evacuating him down the mountain, a 90-minute trip. Partway down, you notice his headache is getting worse, he's more and more drowsy, and one pupil looks slightly larger than the other. HR 52, BP 158/95.
What's going on: A deteriorating TBI with signs of increased intracranial pressure: a worsening headache, drowsiness (a falling level of consciousness), unequal pupils (anisocoria), and Cushing's triad (hypertension, bradycardia). This is a medical emergency.

What you do now:
  • Stop the slow ground carry and escalate to the fastest evacuation available.
  • Call for a helicopter or emergency rescue immediately.
  • Raise the head of the stretcher 30° to help blood drain from the head (venous drainage) and reduce ICP.
  • Keep him calm and cool, with as little exertion as possible.
  • Watch his airway. He's at risk of losing it.
  • Monitor his vitals and pupils constantly.
  • Be ready to manage an airway emergency.
  • Do not delay the evacuation. Without hospital care, brain herniation is imminent.
Unequal pupils after a head injury are a neurosurgical emergency. Brain herniation can happen fast.

Summary & Key Takeaways

The short version:
  • A concerning mechanism or symptoms means you keep the patient still and evacuate.
  • Discontinuing spine precautions is a decision for higher-trained rescuers or medical direction. It isn't a WFA skill.
  • A logroll takes at least 3 rescuers, and the patient rolls as one unit.
  • The goal is spinal motion restriction (SMR): generous padding and blanket rolls. Rigid collars are de-emphasized and never enough on their own.
  • Never apply traction to a suspected spine injury. Support the head in neutral without pulling.
  • Helmet on if breathing is adequate, the airway is accessible, and the fit is snug. Helmet off if you can't manage the airway, the patient is vomiting or needs rescue breathing or CPR, or the helmet is loose or blocks neutral alignment. A full-face chin bar blocks all airway management.
  • Helmet removal is a two-rescuer job. Stabilization gets handed off, never dropped. No traction. Pad under the occiput once a full-face helmet comes off.
  • Cushing's triad (severe hypertension with a widening pulse pressure, bradycardia, irregular respirations) means a raised-ICP emergency.
  • Unequal pupils after a TBI are a neurosurgical emergency.
  • A concussion is a disruption of brain function. It doesn't require unconsciousness.
  • Concussion care: relative rest for 24-48 hours, then a gradual, symptom-limited return. Sleep is allowed. Check overnight that they can be roused, and never leave them alone.

Glossary of Terms

Anisocoria
Unequal pupil size. After a head injury, it can mean brain herniation or increased intracranial pressure.
Basilar Skull Fracture
A fracture at the base of the skull. It can be subtle, but it's serious. Signs include Battle's sign, raccoon eyes, and leaking CSF.
Cauda Equina Syndrome
A neurological emergency from compression of the bundle of nerve roots in the lumbar spine. It causes leg paralysis and bowel and bladder dysfunction.
Cervical Spine
The neck part of the spine (C1-C7). The most mobile and least stable region, and injuries here are often catastrophic.
Concussion
A type of traumatic brain injury in which a blow to the head disrupts normal brain function. There may or may not be a loss of consciousness.
Cushing's Triad
Hypertension, bradycardia, and irregular respirations. A late sign of dangerously increased intracranial pressure.
CSF (Cerebrospinal Fluid)
The clear fluid around the brain and spinal cord. If it's leaking from the nose or ears, that indicates a basilar skull fracture.
Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)
A 3-to-15 score for level of consciousness, based on eye opening, verbal response, and motor response.
Herniation
Brain tissue shifting under increased intracranial pressure. It can kill quickly, and unequal pupils are the sign.
Logroll
Moving a patient with a suspected spinal injury as one unit, with the spine kept straight. Takes at least 3 rescuers.
Occiput
The back of the skull. After a full-face helmet comes off, pad under the occiput to keep the head in neutral alignment, because the helmet had been holding the head up.
Postconcussion Syndrome
Symptoms (headache, cognitive dysfunction, mood changes) that last weeks to months after a concussion.
Spinal Cord
The bundle of nerves running through the vertebral column. Damage at any level causes loss of function below that level.
Spinal Motion Restriction (SMR)
Keeping the spine still with generous padding, blanket rolls, and careful handling, instead of relying on rigid devices. It's the modern goal of field spine care.
Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI)
Injury to the brain from a blow or a violent movement. It ranges from mild (concussion) to severe (unconsciousness, permanent disability).
Wilderness Spine Assessment (WSA)
Reference criteria higher-trained rescuers use to decide whether spine precautions can be discontinued in the field. At the WFA level, keep the patient still and evacuate.
