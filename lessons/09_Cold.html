Overview: Cold Emergencies

Cold injuries run from frostbite (frozen tissue) to hypothermia (a dangerous drop in core body temperature). Out here, cold exposure can turn life-threatening, especially in wet, windy conditions up high or in the mountains.

"No one is dead until warm and dead." Even a severely hypothermic patient with no pulse you can find may be resuscitated with rewarming.

Key points

Hypothermia is a core temperature under 35°C (95°F). It's a medical emergency, and it needs evacuation.
Frostbite is frozen tissue. Don't rewarm it in the field if it could refreeze.
Afterdrop: core temperature keeps falling after the patient is out of the cold, as cold blood returns to the core.
CPR in wilderness hypothermia: keep going, even after a long stretch without a pulse.
Prevention: dress in layers, stay dry, and watch your partners for early signs.

Hypothermia: Dangerous Cold

Definition and physiology

  • Hypothermia: core body temperature under 35°C (95°F)
  • Normal core temp: 37°C (98.6°F)
  • Why it's dangerous: a low core temperature slows every metabolic process. The heart gets irritable and can slip into ventricular fibrillation (VF), a deadly arrhythmia. Brain function is impaired.

How the body loses heat in the cold

  • Conduction: direct contact with something cold, like the ground, water, or a cold object. Water pulls heat away 25 times faster than air.
  • Convection: wind strips away the insulating layer of warm air next to the body. Wind chill sharply increases heat loss.
  • Radiation: the body radiates heat into the cold around it
  • Evaporation: wet clothing lets heat escape fast through evaporation
  • Respiration: breathing in cold air and breathing out warm air costs heat
Water and wind do the most damage. Immersion in cold water (conduction), and wind on top of wet clothing, cause rapid heat loss.

Hypothermia staging

Field staging (no thermometer). WFA rescuers can't measure core temperature, so stage hypothermia by what you can see. The numeric ranges further down are for reference only.

Functional field staging

  • Cold-stressed (not hypothermic): alert, shivering, able to look after themselves → food, dry layers, exercise to rewarm.
  • Mild: alert but impaired, shivering hard → shelter, dry insulation, sugar and calories; keep moving only if fully alert.
  • Moderate: decreased consciousness, shivering weak or stopped → handle gently, no walking, insulate and wrap, external heat to the trunk, evacuate.
  • Severe: unconscious → treat them as fragile, full hypothermia wrap, urgent evacuation, be ready for CPR.

Numeric reference ranges

Mild hypothermia (32-35°C / 90-95°F)

  • Signs:
    • Shivering. A good sign: the body is still trying to warm itself.
    • Poor coordination (ataxia, clumsiness)
    • Slurred speech, confusion
    • Pale or bluish (cyanotic) skin
    • Normal to slightly high blood pressure
  • Patient awareness: usually aware of their condition, and may look for shelter

Moderate hypothermia (28-32°C / 82-90°F)

  • Signs:
    • Shivering stops. A bad sign: the body has quit trying to make heat.
    • More confusion and poor judgment. They may refuse to move or to get help.
    • Trouble walking (ataxia) and lost fine motor control
    • Slow heart rate (bradycardia) and low blood pressure (hypotension)
    • Paradoxical undressing: the patient feels warm and takes clothes off. This is dangerous.
  • How paradoxical undressing happens: the peripheral vessels dilate, and the confused patient feels a sudden rush of warmth while the core keeps dropping. They undress, and heat loss speeds up.

Severe hypothermia (<28°C / <82°F)

  • Signs:
    • Unconscious or barely conscious
    • No shivering (the muscles have stopped)
    • Dilated pupils (they can look dead)
    • Extreme bradycardia: the pulse may be barely detectable, under 30 bpm
    • Extreme hypotension, possibly unmeasurable
    • May look dead, and may not be
  • Ventricular fibrillation risk: the heart may be in VF and won't respond to defibrillation until the core temperature comes up

Afterdrop: the complication

What afterdrop is: core temperature keeps falling even after the patient is out of the cold and rewarming has started

  • Mechanism: the core keeps cooling as its heat conducts out into the cold tissue around it. Movement adds a circulatory effect, as cold blood from the limbs mixes back toward the core.
  • Rescue collapse (a separate problem): cardiovascular collapse around the time of rescue, triggered by rough handling or position changes, like standing the patient up. That's why gentle, horizontal handling matters.
  • How to minimize it:
    • Gentle, horizontal handling. No vigorous exercise, no rough movement, no sitting or standing them up.
    • Don't rub the arms and legs hard
    • Don't rewarm the arms and legs before the core
    • Put external heat on the trunk (chest, armpits, groin), not the arms and legs
Handle with care. Treat a severely hypothermic patient as if they're in unstable VF. Rough handling can trigger an arrhythmia.

Field treatment by stage

All hypothermia stages

  • Remove wet clothing, if that's what it takes to stop ongoing evaporative loss
  • Insulate them from the ground (sleeping pad, branches, packs). This ground layer is insulation, not a vapor barrier.
  • Add insulation on top (sleeping bag, blankets, clothing)
  • Add external heat:
    • Hot water bottles or heat packs against the trunk: groin, armpits (axillae), chest. Not too hot, and wrapped so they don't touch skin directly.
    • Wrap them in blankets or other insulating material
    • Skin-to-skin contact from another person mostly prevents further heat loss. It transfers little heat, so pair it with insulation and wrapped heat sources.
  • Offer warm, sweet drinks if they're conscious and swallowing normally

Moderate hypothermia (28-32°C)

  • Everything above
  • Handle them gently. Rough handling can trigger VF.
  • Lay them flat on their back (supine)
  • No alcohol
  • Watch the airway carefully

Severe hypothermia (<28°C)

  • Everything above, but even more gently
  • Handle them as if they're in unstable VF
  • Protect the airway; don't force anything by mouth
  • CPR if there's no pulse (see below)
  • Build a hypothermia wrap (a "burrito," in trail slang), layered from the patient outward:
    • Dry the patient (remove or replace wet clothing)
    • Vapor barrier (plastic sheet or tarp) against the clothing
    • Thick insulation all the way around, including underneath the patient
    • Windproof, waterproof outer shell
    • Head covered, face left exposed
    • Wrapped heat sources placed at the trunk
  • Urgent evacuation. They may need active core rewarming in a hospital.

Hypothermic cardiac arrest: "No one is dead until warm and dead"

  • Principle: a severely hypothermic patient can go a long time with no detectable pulse and still be alive and resuscitable
  • CPR guidelines:
    • Check the carotid pulse for up to 60 seconds. Hypothermia slows the pulse.
    • If there's any pulse or sign of life after that 60-second check, even a very slow one, do not start chest compressions
    • No pulse or sign of life: start CPR
    • Keep CPR going through the whole evacuation
    • If you suspect hypothermia, do not withhold CPR because the arrest has gone on a long time
    • Accepted exceptions: don't start or continue CPR if there's an obvious lethal injury, the chest is frozen solid and can't be compressed, the airway is packed with snow or ice, or there's a valid DNR order
  • Defibrillation: the heart may be in VF and won't respond to defibrillation until core temp comes up. If a defibrillator is available, attempt defibrillation once, but don't repeat it until core temp is above 30°C.
  • Active core rewarming: the hospital has warmed IV fluids, heated humidified oxygen, and ECMO (extracorporeal membrane oxygenation) for severe cases. Some patients have recovered after hours without a pulse when hypothermia was the cause.
Keep going. A hypothermic patient who looks dead may still be alive, so continue CPR. There are recovery stories of patients revived after more than 3 hours of cardiac arrest when hypothermia was the cause.

Frostbite & Cold Injuries

Frostbite: frozen tissue

What: ice crystals forming in tissue cells at freezing temperatures

  • Mechanism: tissue freezes when its temperature drops below freezing and ice crystals form. Cold-induced vasoconstriction speeds the cooling, but it isn't what freezes the tissue. The ice crystals then damage cells, both mechanically and osmotically.
  • Most common sites: fingers, toes, nose, ears, cheeks
  • Risk factors:
    • Wet skin or wet clothing
    • Wind chill
    • Immobility (can't move to make heat)
    • Dehydration, malnutrition
    • Prior frostbite (the skin loses cold tolerance)

Frostbite stages

Frostnip (superficial cold injury)

  • What: superficial tissue damage, with no actual freezing of deeper tissue
  • Signs: skin white or pale, numb, no blisters
  • Recovery: rapid and complete with rewarming (30 min). Full function comes back.
  • Treatment: rewarm gently with body heat or warm water

Superficial frostbite

  • What: the skin is frozen, and the deeper tissue is still soft
  • Signs: white or waxy while frozen. Clear blisters (serous fluid) form after rewarming. They're the less serious kind.
  • Healing: slower, and it can take weeks. Usually a full recovery, though cold tolerance may be permanently reduced.
  • Treatment: rewarm (see below)

Deep frostbite (full thickness)

  • What: skin and deep tissue frozen (muscle, bone). The most serious kind.
  • Signs: hard and wooden to the touch. Mottled blue or purple. Blood-filled blisters after rewarming, which point to tissue death.
  • Prognosis: tissue death is likely. Expect permanent damage, and it may mean amputation.
  • Treatment: rewarm, then urgent evacuation for surgery

The rewarming decision

Only rewarm if there's no risk of refreezing. Thawing and then refreezing does far more damage than waiting to rewarm.
  • Decision: if there's a risk the tissue will refreeze, leave the frostbitten area frozen
  • Why: refreezing causes massive additional tissue damage as ice crystals form again
  • Example: a hiker with frozen toes, deep in the backcountry, 8 hours from help. Walking out on frozen feet beats thawing them and having them refreeze on the way.

Rewarming technique: when to rewarm

  • Method: warm water immersion, not dry heat
  • Temperature: 37-39°C, body-temperature warm, not hot. No boiling water.
  • Duration: 30-45 minutes of immersion. Thawing takes time.
  • Pain: rewarming is very painful. Acknowledge it, and do what you can to keep them comfortable.
  • Activity: gentle. Don't massage or rub the tissue while it rewarms.

Post-thaw care

  • Don't:
    • Rub the tissue (the cells are fragile)
    • Apply direct heat (fire, stove)
    • Use alcohol or massage
  • Do:
    • Leave every blister intact in the field, including the blood-filled (hemorrhagic) ones
    • Protect blisters with dry, bulky dressings. Draining a blister is beyond field care.
    • Elevate the affected area to reduce swelling
    • Pad or splint it gently for comfort
    • Pain relief (ibuprofen also helps reduce inflammation)
  • Evacuation: all significant frostbite needs medical evaluation

Other cold injuries

Chilblain (pernio)

  • Chronic exposure to cold and wet at non-freezing temperatures
  • Itchy, red or purple lesions on the ears, fingers, and toes
  • Treatment: rewarm and keep dry

Immersion foot (trench foot)

  • Long wet, cold exposure (hours to days) without freezing
  • Maceration (softened, waterlogged skin), pain, blisters, possible gangrene
  • Treatment: clean and dry the feet, and rewarm slowly
  • Prevention: keep feet dry on cold, wet expeditions

Field Rewarming Strategies

Passive external rewarming

  • Mild hypothermia: this may be enough
  • Method: insulate them from the cold with blankets, a sleeping bag, clothing
  • Let the body make its own heat: shivering generates heat, as long as the insulation holds it in
  • Advantage: no risk of afterdrop from active rewarming

Active external rewarming

  • Methods:
    • Hot water bottles, wrapped and placed on the core, not the arms and legs. The most accessible field method.
    • Heat packs (chemical, or stove-warmed rocks wrapped in cloth)
    • Warm drinks if they're conscious and able to swallow
    • Skin-to-skin contact with a rescuer. It mostly prevents further heat loss rather than transferring much heat. Insulation plus wrapped external heat (warm water bottles or heat packs against the trunk) works better.
  • Target areas: the core (chest, armpits, groin), where major blood vessels run close to the skin
  • Caution: go gently, and don't burn the skin

Active internal rewarming

  • Field options:
    • Warm oral fluids (if they're conscious and can swallow). They work better with sugar in them, which fuels metabolic heat.
    • That's about the limit in the field, without advanced equipment
  • Hospital options:
    • Warmed IV fluids
    • Heated humidified oxygen
    • ECMO (extracorporeal membrane oxygenation) for severe cases, the most advanced option

Rewarming complications

  • Afterdrop: a rapid drop in core temperature after rewarming starts, as cold blood from the limbs returns to the core
  • Dehydration: cold cuts fluid intake, and rewarming adds fluid loss through sweating
  • Dysrhythmias: the heart can develop arrhythmias during rewarming
  • Prevention: rewarm gently, core first, and keep monitoring

Prevention of Cold Injuries

Clothing: the layering system

  • Layer 1 (base): moisture-wicking material (merino wool, synthetic), not cotton. Cotton holds moisture and loses its insulation when wet.
  • Layer 2 (insulation): fleece, wool, or down. It traps air. Wool and fleece keep insulating when damp; down loses it quickly once wet.
  • Layer 3 (shell): a windproof, waterproof outer layer. It keeps out the wind chill and the wet.
  • Extremities: a hat (cuts heat loss through the head), gloves, socks, gaiters to keep snow out
Save the cotton for the drive home. Choose wool or synthetic for any layer against the skin. Even in cool, not cold, weather, cotton loses all its insulation once it's wet from sweat or water.

Hydration and nutrition

  • Hydration: cold blunts the thirst mechanism, so it's easy to get dehydrated. Drink regularly.
  • Nutrition: metabolism is the main source of heat. Eat high-calorie food to fuel it.
  • Carbohydrates: quick energy, burned for heat
  • Fats: sustained energy and insulation

Monitoring and prevention

  • Early signs in yourself: shivering you can't control, poor coordination, confusion. Get to shelter and warmth immediately.
  • Early signs in a partner: shivering, slurred speech, clumsiness, withdrawal. Someone getting hypothermic often doesn't recognize their own symptoms.
  • Buddy system: never hike alone in the cold. A partner can spot the early signs and act on them.
  • Frequent breaks: don't push through cold and fatigue. Take breaks in shelter to rewarm.
  • Avoid alcohol: it causes vasodilation (more heat loss) and impairs judgment

Environmental management

  • Shelter: get out of the wind and precipitation. Even a natural windbreak (rocks, trees) helps a lot.
  • Insulation: don't sit on cold ground. Put a pad, branches, or a pack under you.
  • Fire: if you have one and it's safe, it's a major source of warmth. Don't rely on fire alone; you still need insulation.
  • Avoid immersion: stay dry. If you get wet, change into dry clothes immediately.

Case Scenarios

Scenario 1: Wet hiker showing signs of hypothermia

Setting: A hiker has been out in cold rain and wind for 3 hours. She's shivering violently and confused, and she refuses to stop. Her skin is pale. You're 6 hours from the trailhead, in exposed mountain terrain.

Recognition: Mild hypothermia (likely 32-35°C). Shivering, confusion, and wet: the classic presentation.

What you do now:

  • Stop moving and get to shelter immediately: behind rocks, trees, any windbreak
  • Get the wet clothing off if you can, and replace it with dry
  • Insulate her from the ground with whatever you've got
  • Add blankets, a sleeping bag, more layers
  • Passive external rewarming: let her own heat do the work
  • Warm, sweet drinks if she's conscious

Evacuation: If she improves, and her shivering stops for the right reason (she's getting warm), she may be able to walk out slowly. If her confusion gets worse or she becomes unresponsive, she needs an urgent evacuation.

Scenario 2: Frostbitten fingers on a high peak

Setting: A climber high on a peak (14,000 ft) develops white, numb fingers at -10°C in the wind. It's a 4-6 hour descent to base camp. The fingers are clearly frostbitten.

Recognition: Frostbite developing (white, numb fingers), with a risk of refreezing on the descent.

The call: Do not rewarm on the mountain. The risk of refreezing is too high.

Field management:

  • Insulate the fingers: in pockets, under the armpits, in gloves (keep them frozen)
  • Guard against any further cold exposure with extreme care
  • Keep the fingers dry
  • Descend carefully to shelter

At base camp or shelter: Then rewarm with warm water immersion (37-39°C), if you're certain there's no risk of refreezing. Urgent evacuation to a hospital for frostbite care.

Lesson Summary

The short version

Hypothermia is a core temp under 35°C (95°F). Handle them gently so you don't trigger an arrhythmia.
Shivering is good: the body is making heat. Shivering that stops without rewarming is bad: the body has quit.
Afterdrop: core temp keeps falling after they're out of the cold. Gentle handling helps prevent it.
No one is dead until warm and dead. Keep up CPR in hypothermia, even after a long cardiac arrest.
Frostbite: don't rewarm in the field if it could refreeze.
Rewarming frostbite: a warm water bath at 37-39°C, for 30-45 minutes.
Prevention: skip the cotton, layer properly, stay dry, use the buddy system.

Glossary

Hypothermia
Core body temperature under 35°C (95°F). A medical emergency that needs careful rewarming.
Afterdrop
Core temperature that keeps falling after the patient is out of the cold, as cold blood returns to the core
Shivering
Involuntary muscle contractions that make heat. Present in mild and moderate hypothermia, absent in severe.
Frostnip
Superficial cold injury without actual tissue freezing. Reversible with rewarming.
Frostbite
Frozen tissue: ice crystals form and damage the cells
Rewarming
Bringing core body temperature back up from hypothermia. It has to be done carefully to avoid complications.
Paradoxical Undressing
Taking clothes off in hypothermia, because peripheral vasodilation brings a sensation of warmth
Chilblain
A cold injury from chronic exposure to non-freezing cold and wet
Immersion Foot
A cold injury from prolonged wet, cold exposure without actual freezing
Ventricular Fibrillation
A chaotic heart rhythm. Rough handling of a severely hypothermic patient can trigger it.
Insulation
Material that traps air and prevents heat loss (clothing, sleeping bag, ground barrier)
