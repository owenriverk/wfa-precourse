Evacuation & Patient Transport

Making sound decisions and executing safe evacuation in the wilderness

Lesson Overview

Learning objectives

  • Use a decision framework to sort an evacuation into urgent, non-urgent, or not needed
  • Package a patient for transport, with immobilization and protection from hypothermia
  • Build a safe improvised litter and run a team carry
  • Activate emergency communication devices and give rescuers the patient information they need
  • Prepare a landing zone and stay safe around a helicopter

Evacuation as a core wilderness medicine skill

Evacuation is where knowledge meets judgment and improvisation. Out here your transport options are limited, and you work with what you've got. Your calls on urgency, method, and timing decide whether the patient reaches definitive care in time, whether complications set in, and ultimately how it ends. Evacuation isn't the step after the medicine. Deciding how this patient gets out (walking, litter, helicopter) is the medicine. Some patients need a helicopter now. Others can walk out slowly with a little support.

Evacuation Decision Framework

Core principle: urgency × feasibility

Every evacuation decision weighs two things: how urgent the patient's condition is, and what it'll practically take to get them out. A life-threatening condition means an urgent evacuation, however hard the terrain. A stable patient with a minor injury might walk out, or wait for the weather to improve.

Evacuation urgency categories

Category Timeline Examples Notes
Urgent Within hours Life threats, a deteriorating patient, unstable circulation (hemodynamic instability), altered mental status (AMS), spine injury, severe hypothermia or heat stroke, suspected ACS or stroke, anaphylaxis, open fractures, compartment syndrome, complete amputation Every minute matters. Activate rescue immediately.
Non-urgent Within 12-24 hours Stable closed fractures, early signs of infection, controlled bleeding, moderate environmental illness, wounds that need closing Can go by litter or assisted walk without immediate danger of getting worse.
Self-evacuate / no evacuation Patient's discretion Minor sprains (can bear weight), superficial wounds, mild GI illness, fatigue Teach the patient the red flags that would change this decision.

Decision factors

Patient condition & trend

Is the patient stable, improving, or getting worse? Where they're heading matters more than how bad they look right now. A patient who's worsening gets an urgent evacuation, whatever their current presentation.

Terrain & distance

How far is definitive care? How long will the trip take? Narrow passes, elevation changes, and water crossings all add time and difficulty.

Resources available

How many trained rescuers do you have? What equipment? Any communication devices? With limited resources, you may have to evacuate to a designated place rather than to the nearest hospital.

Environment & weather

What's the forecast? How much daylight is left? Is cold, a storm, or darkness coming? The environment can demand a faster evacuation even over a short distance.

Keep reassessing

The evacuation category can change. Reassess every 15 minutes in a stable patient and every 5 minutes in an unstable one. A non-urgent patient who develops respiratory distress or altered mental status is now urgent. Record each assessment so you can see the trend.

When in doubt, evacuate

Out here there's no observation unit. You can't admit anyone, order blood work, or escalate care. If you aren't sure an evacuation is needed, evacuate. It's easier to stand down a rescue that's reached the trailhead than to call for an urgent one after dark in a storm.

Preparing a Patient for Transport

Documentation: SOAP note for handoff

Finish a SOAP note (Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan) before EMS reaches the trailhead or the helicopter lands. Include the chief complaint, mechanism of injury, vital signs, treatments given, how the patient responded, and your assessment of how stable they are. It tells receiving providers what they're getting, so nothing gets missed at handoff.

Hypothermia prevention during transport

Immobility plus shock, pain, and exposure speeds up hypothermia. Insulate aggressively, even in moderate weather.

Ground insulation

Put 3-4 layers of insulation between the patient and the ground: sleeping pads, sleeping bags, packs, vegetation. Do not let them lie directly on cold ground or snow.

Vapor barrier

Wrap the patient in plastic sheeting or garbage bags to trap warm air. This vapor barrier stops evaporative heat loss and keeps the wind off.

Head coverage

Heat loss tracks exposed surface area, and the head is roughly 10% of the body's surface, not the 30-40% of popular myth. Cover it anyway. It has good blood flow, it's often left bare, and it's one of the easiest places to insulate. A wool hat, balaclava, or improvised headwear meaningfully cuts overall heat loss.

External heat sources

Bottles of warm (not boiling) water placed against the groin, armpits (axillae), and neck add external warmth. Insulate the bottles so they don't burn the skin.

Insulating shell

Wrap the patient in sleeping bags plus extra clothing or blankets to build an insulating shell.

Securing the patient in the litter

Once they're in the litter, strap them in so they can't fall or shift.

Padding & positioning

  • Pad every bony prominence: back of the head (occiput), sacrum, heels, elbows
  • Position for the injury (see the table below)

Strapping

  • Chest strap: above the nipple line, so they can breathe
  • Waist/hip strap: secures the pelvis
  • Thigh straps: stop them sliding
  • Arms: padded, and secured at the sides or across the chest
  • Head: padding on both sides (rolled sleeping pads) stops it moving side to side

Recheck the ABCs after packaging, every time. Before you start moving, confirm the airway is open, breathing is adequate, and circulation is present.

Transport positions

Condition Recommended Position
Unconscious, no mechanism for spine injury Recovery position (on their side, head tilted back to keep the airway open). When in doubt, treat it as a spine injury.
Spinal precautions Flat on the back (supine) with the spine neutral, secured on a spine board in the litter
Respiratory distress Semi-recumbent (head raised about 45°) to ease breathing
Shock Flat on the back (supine). Raise the feet 6–12 in only if there's no trauma and it doesn't hurt. Insulate above and below, with heat sources at the trunk.
Nausea/vomiting risk Recovery position or semi-lateral, to prevent aspiration
General comfort (no specific concern) Position of comfort, whatever the patient prefers

Medication monitoring

Write down every medication given: drug name, dose, time, route (swallowed, chewed, inhaled, auto-injector), and how the patient responded. Receiving providers need it, and it prevents dangerous double dosing.

Vital sign monitoring during transport

Recheck vitals every 5 minutes in an unstable patient and every 15 minutes in a stable one. Record each set so the trend shows. Any change in mental status, breathing, heart rate, or perfusion means you check more often and ask whether the evacuation needs upgrading.

Improvised Litter Construction

When you need an improvised litter

No helicopter, a long way to the trailhead, or a patient who can't walk even with help. Without a commercial stretcher, an improvised litter is an essential wilderness skill.

It has to be rigid

A litter that sags is a hammock with handles: uncomfortable for the patient and exhausting to carry. Build something that holds the patient up without sagging.

Improvised litter methods

Method Materials Pros Cons
Jacket + poles 2-3 jackets, 2 sturdy poles (branches, ski poles, ice axes) Easy, uses common gear, good for moderate distances Less rigid; limited for larger patients
Tarp/poncho litter Tarp or poncho, 2 rigid poles Very strong, large surface area, good for rough terrain Needs a tarp; can be heavy
Two-backpack litter 2 large packs, poles lashed through the pack frames or straps Sturdy, good padding from the pack frames, materials usually on hand Heavier than the other options
Blanket/sleeping bag roll Sleeping bag or blanket, poles run through the sides Excellent insulation, quick to build, light Less rigid; limited for long distances

Running the carry team

Team size

  • Ideal: 6 carriers (2 at the head, 2 at the middle/waist, 2 at the feet) spread the weight and lift evenly
  • Minimum: 4 carriers (2 at each end, on opposite sides)

Commands

One team leader at the head calls clear commands: "Prepare to lift," "Lift," "Step," "Halt." That keeps everyone moving together and prevents injuries.

Rotation

Rotate carriers every 10-15 minutes to head off fatigue and keep the litter steady. Tired carriers make mistakes and risk dropping the litter.

Pace and communication

  • Keep a steady, slow pace. Speed isn't possible on uneven ground, and trying raises the risk of falls.
  • Call out obstacles ahead of time: "Rock on right," "Step down," "Creek crossing ahead"
  • Assign one person just to watch the patient's comfort and stability during the carry

Narrow trail technique

On single-file trail, carriers can stagger their feet (one foot on each side of the trail, alternating) to get the litter through narrow sections. It's harder than it sounds and takes practice.

One-rescuer carries (short distances only)

For a short move, such as an injured person near a shelter or campsite, one rescuer can carry the patient with the right technique. Solo carries are only for patients with no suspected spine injury, and only when immediate danger forces the move. You can't monitor the airway of an unconscious patient draped over your shoulder, so treat these carries as a last resort:

  • Fireman's carry: for an unconscious or larger patient, draped across your shoulders.
  • Cradle carry: for a small patient or a short distance. You hold them across your chest and arms.
  • Walking assist: for an alert patient who can walk. Their arm around your neck, your arm around their waist.

Helicopter considerations

If a helicopter's likely, confirm litter orientation with the rescue team. Litters have to go into the aircraft in the direction it's positioned, typically feet first. Make sure your litter can be loaded that way.

Emergency Communication in the Wilderness

Communication devices compared

Cell phone

Pros: Everyone has one. Cons: Coverage gaps, battery drain. Tips: Hold the phone high, try 911 even with no bars (it may reach any tower), and try a text when calls fail.

Satellite communicator (Garmin InReach, SPOT)

Pros: Two-way messaging anywhere, GPS tracking. Cons: Needs a subscription; SOS response is slower than a voice call.

PLB (personal locator beacon)

Pros: Free monitoring through COSPAS-SARSAT, no subscription, watched worldwide by rescue authorities. Cons: One-way only (nobody can talk back), and hard to cancel once it's activated.

Satellite phone

Pros: Voice calls from anywhere. Cons: Expensive, less portable, slower to connect.

MIST report: what rescuers need

When you reach emergency services, give a MIST report plus the key logistics:

  • M = Mechanism of injury: How did it happen? A fall, avalanche, lightning, animal attack?
  • I = Injuries found: The chief complaint and the injuries you've found. "Open femur fracture, ankle swelling, intact neurovascular."
  • S = Signs and symptoms: Vital signs, mental status, pain level, relevant history (diabetic, allergies, medications).
  • T = Treatment given so far: Fluids, medications, splinting, wound care. "Bandaged wound, splinted ankle, gave aspirin."
  • Plus: exact GPS coordinates, if you have a GPS device
  • Plus: number of patients and number of rescuers on scene
  • Plus: resources available (litter, communication device, medical equipment)
  • Plus: weather conditions and forecast
  • Plus: ETA to the trailhead, if you're self-evacuating

Helicopter landing zone preparation

Minimum clear area

100 × 100 feet (30 × 30 meters) of fairly flat ground, clear of debris, branches, and loose material that could get sucked into the rotors or blown into faces.

Marking the LZ

  • Mark it with an X that's visible from the air, in bright colors (surveyor tape, sleeping bags, packs)
  • Take off hats and secure loose items that rotor downwash will blow around
  • Keep all bystanders back 200+ feet from the LZ

Signaling

  • Arms raised in a Y: "Yes, land here"
  • One arm down, one arm up (an L shape): "No, do not land"

Staying safe around the aircraft

  • Never approach a helicopter from the rear. The spinning tail rotor is nearly invisible and lethal. Approach only from the front or side, within the crew's line of sight, and only after they signal you in. Stay low and secure loose items.
  • The signaler stands at the edge of the LZ with their back to the wind. The aircraft lands into the wind, facing the signaler.
  • No fires or smoke downwind of the LZ
  • Loading the patient: follow the crew's instructions exactly. They run the loading.

Device testing & false activations

Before every trip, test your communication device's battery and function. Know which button is SOS and which sends an ordinary message, and learn that at home, not by pressing it. False activations set off expensive rescue operations and carry serious legal consequences. Never activate an emergency device as a test in the field.

Alert rescue early, not late

It's easier to stand down a rescue that reaches the trailhead than to launch one after dark in a storm. If you're unsure whether you need an evacuation, alert rescuers early. Give them your location and situation, and let them advise whether a formal rescue is needed.

Scenario-Based Learning

Scenario 1: Open Femur Fracture in the Canyon

Setting: A climber falls 15 feet in a slot canyon and has an obvious open femur fracture. You're 8 miles from the trailhead. It's 3 PM, with 4 hours of daylight left. You have a satellite communicator, a first aid kit, two sleeping pads, and 3 other group members.

Think it through:

  • What's the evacuation urgency?
  • What do you do in the next 10 minutes?
  • Do you self-evacuate or call for rescue?
Working it through:
  • Urgency: urgent. An open fracture means contaminated bone, a risk of major bleeding and of shock, and a lot of pain. It can't wait.
  • Next 10 minutes:
    • Control any bleeding (direct pressure, pressure dressing). If the bleeding is arterial and uncontrolled, put a tourniquet on the thigh 2-3 inches above (proximal to) the bleeding site, or "high and tight" near the groin if you can't tell the wound's full extent. Never place a tourniquet below the wound.
    • Do not reduce (realign) the fracture. Support it in the position you found it.
    • Gently irrigate the exposed bone and wound with clean water, and cover it with a loose sterile dressing.
    • Splint the femur with the sleeping pads, clothing, or an improvised splint. Immobilize from hip to ankle.
    • Check neurovascular status below the fracture: pulses, capillary refill, sensation, color. If the foot is pulseless or numb, the evacuation is emergent.
    • Nothing by mouth. This patient may need surgery, and shock is a real risk.
  • Evacuation decision: Don't attempt a litter carry over 8 miles with 3 rescuers. The terrain is extremely difficult, the patient is badly hurt, and the risk of further injury on the way out is high. Activate the satellite communicator now: "Open femur fracture, 8 miles from trailhead, 4 adults, urgent evacuation needed. GPS: [coordinates]. Patient immobilized, bleeding controlled, needs helicopter or SAR with additional personnel."
  • While you wait for rescue:
    • Check vitals every 5 minutes. Shock is a major risk.
    • Hypothermia prevention matters here: ground insulation, vapor barrier, head coverage.
    • Manage pain without medication: good splinting, padding, and gentle handling. Don't give NSAIDs when significant bleeding is a concern, and this patient is nothing by mouth anyway. Local wound care only, no suturing.
    • Keep them as warm and comfortable as you can
    • Write the SOAP note: mechanism, vital signs, treatments given, how the patient responded
  • When the helicopter arrives: Clear the LZ, mark it with an X, and follow the crew's loading instructions.
Scenario 2: Remote River Medical Emergency

Setting: Day 5 of a 7-day river trip. A 58-year-old in the group develops crushing chest pain that spreads to the left arm, and they're sweating (diaphoresis). There's no satellite communicator, but you have a PLB. The nearest takeout is 2 days away by river.

Think it through:

  • What's your field assessment and treatment?
  • How do you call for help?
  • Which packaging decisions matter?
Working it through:
  • Field assessment: OPQRST for the chest pain: Onset 30 minutes ago, Provocation none, Quality crushing, Radiation to the left arm, Severity 8/10, Time ongoing. Also present: sweating (diaphoresis), anxiety, possible shortness of breath. SAMPLE: history of high blood pressure, takes aspirin and lisinopril, last ate 2 hours ago. Vitals: BP, HR, and RR all elevated.
  • A strong cardiac picture: treat it as acute coronary syndrome (ACS) until a hospital evaluates them.
  • Field treatment:
    • Aspirin 325 mg, chewed, if there are no contraindications (no allergy, no history of GI bleeding)
    • Position of comfort. Most cardiac patients prefer sitting up slightly.
    • Nothing else by mouth after the aspirin
    • Watch the vital signs closely
  • Calling for help: Activate the PLB now. It triggers COSPAS-SARSAT satellite monitoring, and rescue authorities worldwide are notified within minutes. Your location goes out automatically. But a PLB is one-way. Rescuers can't message you back. The rescue coordination center will contact the beacon's registered emergency contacts for trip details. That's why registering the beacon, and leaving a trip plan with someone at home, matter so much. Don't wait to see if the symptoms improve.
  • Packaging for evacuation:
    • Semi-recumbent (head raised about 45°) for comfort
    • Warm insulation
    • Check ABCs and vitals every 5 minutes. A cardiac event can deteriorate fast.
    • Be ready for a helicopter extraction if that's how rescue arrives
  • Do not paddle out. Physical exertion raises the heart's workload and its need for oxygen. Wait for rescue.
  • Serial assessment: Any change in mental status, respiratory distress, loss of consciousness, or cardiac arrest changes your immediate priorities and means stepping up supportive care.

Lesson Summary

Key takeaways: evacuation & transport

  • The patient's trend, the terrain, your resources, and the environment set the evacuation urgency.
  • Keeping a patient warm in transport means ground insulation, a vapor barrier, head coverage, and external heat sources.
  • You can build a rigid improvised litter from jackets, tarps, backpacks, or sleeping bags.
  • Carry teams need 4-6 rescuers, rotating every 10-15 minutes to prevent fatigue.
  • Satellite communicators and PLBs reach past cell coverage. Test yours before every trip.
  • A MIST report gives rescuers what they need. Add GPS coordinates and logistics.
  • A helicopter landing zone needs to be 100×100 feet, clear, and marked. Never approach from the rear. Come in from the front or side, and only after the crew signals.
  • Alert rescue early, not late. It's easier to stand down than to launch in darkness or a storm.

Glossary of terms

Urgent Evacuation
The patient has a life-threatening condition or is deteriorating, and must reach definitive care within hours.
Non-Urgent Evacuation
The patient is stable but needs medical evaluation within 12-24 hours. Transport by litter or assisted walk is acceptable.
Patient Packaging
Getting a patient ready for transport: padding, strapping, insulation, and positioning.
MIST Report
A standard format for giving rescuers the essential information: Mechanism, Injuries, Signs/symptoms, Treatment.
PLB (Personal Locator Beacon)
A one-way emergency beacon monitored worldwide by COSPAS-SARSAT. No subscription, and no way for rescuers to talk back.
Satellite Communicator
A two-way messaging device that works anywhere. It needs a subscription and lets you communicate with rescue coordinators.
Landing Zone (LZ)
A clear area set aside for a helicopter evacuation. At least 100×100 feet, marked with an X.
Search and Rescue (SAR)
An organized rescue operation, with multiple rescuers and equipment, coordinated with emergency authorities.
Improvised Litter
An emergency stretcher built from whatever's available (jackets, tarps, packs, branches) to carry a patient.
SOAP Note
A documentation format: Subjective (history), Objective (vitals and findings), Assessment (diagnosis), Plan (treatment).
Hypothermia Prevention
The ways you hold body temperature during transport: ground insulation, vapor barrier, head coverage, external heat.
Litter
A stretcher or carrying device for a patient who's immobilized or can't walk.
