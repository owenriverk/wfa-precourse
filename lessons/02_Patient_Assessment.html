Overview: Systematic Patient Assessment

Wilderness first aid runs on a systematic patient assessment: the XABCDE primary survey, then the head-to-toe secondary survey. Together they find the life threats fast and tell you what to treat first when help is far away.

Treat as you find. In wilderness medicine, assessment and treatment happen at the same time. You don't finish the assessment before you treat a life threat.

Why a fixed order

A fixed order means nothing gets missed and the deadly problems surface first. Your findings then drive the evacuation call and the handoff to the next provider. It also gives you something to hold onto when your own heart rate is up.

Key points

Assessment keeps going. Reassess often, and adjust treatment as the patient's condition changes.
Out here, your assessment skills are your diagnostic tools. There's no CT scanner at the trailhead, so use them thoroughly.
Document findings clearly for the handoff to higher-level providers.

Primary Survey: XABCDE

The primary survey finds and treats life-threatening problems. Each step targets an immediate threat to life, and you treat each problem as you find it.

X Exsanguinating hemorrhage
A Airway
B Breathing
C Circulation
D Disability
E Expose/Environment

X = Exsanguinating hemorrhage

What it is: Massive, life-threatening bleeding. You control it before any other assessment step.

  • Life-threatening bleeding is the most preventable cause of death in trauma
  • Look for arterial bleeding: bright red and pulsing, often from a limb or the groin
  • Put direct pressure on it right away, and keep it there
  • If bleeding from a limb is obviously life-threatening, put a tourniquet on immediately. Hold direct pressure while the tourniquet is being readied.
  • Pack wounds in junctional areas where a tourniquet can't go (groin, armpit/axilla). Never pack a neck wound. Use firm, sustained direct pressure instead.
Bleeding before airway. Check and treat exsanguinating hemorrhage before you check the airway. If a patient is actively losing massive amounts of blood, stopping it comes first.

A = Airway

Goal: Make sure the airway is open (patent) and not at risk of getting blocked.

  • Assessment: Look for the chest or belly moving, listen for sounds, feel for air moving
  • Obstructive sounds: Gurgling (fluid or blood), stridor (partial obstruction), snoring (the tongue has relaxed back)

Opening the airway

  • Head-tilt chin-lift: Use it when you don't suspect a spinal injury. Gently tilt the head back and lift the chin. That lifts the tongue off the back of the throat (pharynx).
  • Jaw thrust: Use it when you do suspect a spinal injury. Grip the angles of the jaw and pull it forward without tilting the head. It protects the cervical spine.

Positioning

  • Unconscious, no spinal concern: recovery position on their side, to protect the airway if they vomit. Either side is fine; left is preferred in late pregnancy. Head on the extended arm, top leg bent for stability.
  • Suspected spinal injury: keep them on their back and in line (supine alignment), with stabilization

B = Breathing

Goal: Make sure enough air is moving in and out (ventilation) and enough oxygen is getting in (oxygenation).

  • Respiratory rate: Normal for an adult is 12-20 breaths a minute. Under 10 or over 30 is concerning.
  • Depth: Enough air per breath (tidal volume). You can see the chest rise and fall.
  • Effort: Is it easy, or labored?
  • Look and listen: Watch that both sides of the chest rise and fall equally. Listen near the mouth and chest for abnormal sounds (gurgling, wheezing, stridor).

Signs of respiratory distress

  • Fast breathing (tachypnea) with retractions, the skin pulling inward
  • Using accessory muscles (in the neck and between the ribs)
  • Nasal flaring (the nostrils widen)
  • Tripod position (sitting forward, braced on the hands)
  • Paradoxical motion (the belly goes in as the chest goes out, the opposite of normal)

C = Circulation

Goal: Check heart rate, rhythm, and perfusion (how well blood is getting around).

  • Pulse check: Carotid (neck) if they're unconscious, radial (wrist) if they're conscious
  • Rate: Normal is 60-100 bpm at rest. Under 60 (bradycardia) or over 100 (tachycardia) can mean a problem.
  • Rhythm: Regular or irregular? A regularly irregular pattern can point to specific conditions.
  • Quality: Strong, or thready (weak)? Bounding (forceful)?

Skin assessment (signs of perfusion)

  • Color: Pink (normal), pale (poor perfusion or cold), mottled (shock, severe hypoperfusion), cyanotic (blue, from hypoxia)
  • Temperature: Warm (normal), cool or cold (peripheral vasoconstriction from shock or cold exposure)
  • Moisture: Dry (normal), moist or diaphoretic (sweating from shock, a cardiac event, fear, or exertion)
  • Capillary refill: Press a nail bed until it blanches, then let go. Color should return in under 2 seconds. Slower refill suggests poor perfusion, though cold hands and feet can also slow it.
The shock triad. Pale, cool, sweaty (diaphoretic) skin means shock or poor perfusion. It needs immediate treatment and an urgent evacuation.

D = Disability (neurologic status)

Goal: Quickly check level of consciousness and neurologic function.

AVPU scale

  • Alert: Awake, oriented, and aware of their surroundings
  • Verbal: Responds to your voice, but may be confused
  • Pain: Responds only to a painful stimulus, like a trapezius squeeze (pinch the muscle between the neck and shoulder)
  • Unresponsive: No response to any stimulus

Pupil assessment (PERRL)

  • Pupils Equal: Both the same size
  • Round: Not irregular or fixed in shape
  • Reactive to Light: They shrink (constrict) when you shine a light in and widen (dilate) in the dark

Unequal pupils, fixed pupils, or a slow reaction to light can mean a serious brain injury. You may hear EMS use the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS). AVPU is the wilderness-level tool, and it's enough.

E = Expose/Environment

Goal: Examine the whole patient for injuries without letting them lose heat.

  • Respectfully remove clothing to expose every possible injury site, protecting their privacy as much as you can
  • Look at and feel the entire body, front and back
  • Look for wounds, deformities, swelling, and bruising
  • Prevent hypothermia: Once you've looked, cover them back up with blankets or insulation right away. There's no heated exam room out here.
  • Think about environmental hazards: weather, temperature, and any other dangers on scene

Secondary Survey: Full Head-to-Toe Exam

The secondary survey is a systematic, detailed physical exam. You do it after the life threats are found and stabilized. It finds the injuries that need treatment and guides the evacuation decision.

Head-to-toe exam sequence

Head (DCAP-BTLS)

  • Deformity: Abnormal shape or asymmetry?
  • Contusions: Bruising from trauma?
  • Abrasions: Scrapes or road rash?
  • Punctures/Penetrations: Wounds breaking the skin?
  • Burns: Any thermal injuries?
  • Tenderness: Pain when the area is pressed (palpated)?
  • Lacerations: Cuts?
  • Swelling: Edema?

Neck

  • Feel (palpate) for tenderness, swelling, and any step or deformity along the spine
  • For reference: EMS providers may also check the neck for jugular vein distension or tracheal deviation. Both are late, unreliable signs of serious chest conditions. Your job is to recognize severe breathing difficulty and evacuate urgently.

Chest

  • Look for paradoxical motion: a flail segment, where part of the ribcage moves opposite to the rest of the chest
  • Watch that both sides rise equally. Listen near the mouth and chest for abnormal breathing sounds.
  • Feel for crepitus, a crackling from air in the tissues
  • Check for deformity or swelling

Abdomen

  • Divide the abdomen into four quadrants (right upper, left upper, right lower, left lower)
  • Look for swelling (distension), bruising, and penetrating wounds
  • Gently palpate each quadrant for tenderness
  • Check for guarding (they tighten the muscles when you touch) and rigidity (a hard, boardlike belly)
  • Guarding and rigidity suggest internal bleeding or peritonitis. That's an urgent evacuation.

Pelvis

  • Gently press on the pelvis once to check stability
  • If it's unstable, do not press again. It can worsen the bleeding.
  • If the mechanism of injury suggests a pelvic fracture, don't stress the pelvis at all. Treat based on the mechanism and the signs.
  • Instability suggests a pelvic fracture, which can bleed massively on the inside

Extremities

  • Check each limb for deformity, swelling, wounds, and color
  • Neurovascular (NV) check: Beyond the injury (distal), check circulation (pulses, skin color and temperature), sensation, and motor function (can they move, and squeeze your hands?)
  • CSM check: Color, Sensation, Motor function beyond (distal to) the injury
  • Document NV status carefully. It guides your splinting decisions.

Back

  • Log-roll the patient carefully, keeping the spine in line if you're worried about a spine injury
  • Inspect the back for wounds, bruising, and deformity
  • Feel down the spine for tenderness, or a step or deformity

SAMPLE history

A complete history tells you what the patient's normal health looks like and what led up to this:

  • S = Signs & Symptoms: What's the main complaint? What are they feeling?
    • OPQRST for pain:
      • Onset: When did it start?
      • Provocation: What makes it worse?
      • Quality: Sharp, dull, burning, pressure, aching?
      • Radiation: Does it spread anywhere else?
      • Severity: Pain scale 0-10
      • Time: How long has it been going on?
  • A = Allergies: Medications, foods, anything environmental? Ever had anaphylaxis?
  • M = Medications: Current medications, including over-the-counter supplements?
  • P = Past Medical History: Past surgeries, illnesses, chronic conditions? Anything recent?
  • L = Last Oral Intake: What did they last eat and drink, and when? It matters for vomiting and anesthesia.
  • E = Events: What led up to this? What was the mechanism of injury?

Vital signs to document

  • Pulse: Rate and quality (strong, or weak and thready)
  • Respirations: Rate and depth of breathing
  • Skin: Color, temperature, moisture
  • Level of consciousness: AVPU, and any change
  • Pupils: Equal, round, reactive to light?
  • Temperature: With a thermometer if you have one, otherwise by touch
  • Pain scale: The patient's own rating, 0-10

Building a field impression

Once you've assessed, settle on a working diagnosis, your field impression. It's your best guess at what's wrong, based on:

  • Mechanism of injury (what happened)
  • The patient's signs and symptoms
  • Your exam findings
  • Their history
  • The environment

Write it plainly: "Patient is a 45-year-old male hiker who fell 10 feet onto his left leg. Complains of severe pain. Left leg is swollen, deformed, neurovascularly intact distally. Field impression: left tibia/fibula fracture."

How often to reassess

Patients change. Reassess and document your findings at regular intervals:

  • Stable patient: Reassess every 15 minutes
  • Unstable patient: Reassess every 5 minutes
  • Document the trend: improving, stable, or getting worse? One set of vitals is a snapshot. Two are a trend.
  • Let your reassessment findings guide changes in treatment

Vital Signs & Patient Documentation

A full set of vitals

Vitals are objective. They don't lie, and they often change before the patient starts complaining.
  • Pulse (heart rate):
    • Normal adult: 60-100 bpm at rest
    • For a quick rate, count for 15 seconds and multiply by 4
    • Rate, rhythm (regular or irregular), and quality (strong, or weak and thready)
  • Respirations (breathing rate):
    • Normal adult: 12-20 breaths per minute
    • Count for a full 60 seconds for accuracy. It'll feel longer than it is.
    • Check depth and effort, not just rate
  • Skin:
    • Color: Pink (normal), pale, mottled, cyanotic (blue)
    • Temperature: Warm, cool, cold
    • Moisture: Dry, moist, diaphoretic (sweating)
    • Capillary refill: press a nail bed and release. Color should return in under 2 seconds.
    • Together, these tell you a lot about perfusion and whether they're in shock
  • Level of consciousness:
    • AVPU scale (Alert, Verbal, Pain, Unresponsive)
    • Any decline is a concern, and it means stepping up treatment
  • Pupils:
    • Equal in size? Reacting to light?
    • Unequal pupils can mean a serious brain problem
  • Temperature:
    • Core body temperature, if you have a thermometer
    • Otherwise, a general read: warm, cool, or cold
  • Pain scale:
    • The patient rates it from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain imaginable)
    • Track how it changes after anything you do for the pain

Documentation: SOAP notes

Clear notes keep care continuous when you hand off to higher-level providers. Use the SOAP format:

  • S = Subjective: What the patient tells you
    • Chief complaint
    • History of present illness
    • SAMPLE history
    • The patient's own words
  • O = Objective: What you measure and observe
    • Vital signs
    • Physical examination findings
    • Mechanism of injury
    • Observable signs and symptoms
  • A = Assessment: Your working diagnosis
    • Your field impression, based on everything you've gathered
    • What do you think is wrong?
  • P = Plan: Your treatment and evacuation decision
    • Treatment started, and how they responded
    • Evacuation urgency (urgent or non-urgent)
    • How they'll get out (walking, litter, helicopter)
    • How you'll monitor them during the evacuation

Case Scenarios

Scenario 1: Unresponsive hiker on a remote trail

Setting: Your group finds a 62-year-old male hiker unresponsive on a mountain trail, 6 miles from the trailhead. He has no obvious external injuries and no ID. You have basic first aid training and a satellite communicator.

Primary survey:

  • X: Check for massive bleeding. None visible.
  • A: Check the airway. He's unresponsive, with no sounds of airway obstruction.
  • B: Is he breathing? At what rate? If he is, is it adequate?
  • C: Is there a carotid pulse? Rate and quality?
  • D: AVPU puts him at unresponsive. Check the pupils. Any obvious head trauma?
  • E: Remove clothing to check for injuries. Any signs of trauma? A medical alert bracelet?

Secondary survey:

  • Full head-to-toe exam, looking for the cause
  • If he carries his own glucometer, use it to check his blood glucose. This could be a diabetic emergency.
  • Look for signs of head trauma, stroke, seizure, or medication effects

Documentation: Unresponsive male, name unknown, no identifiable mechanism of injury. Pupils equal and reactive. Breathing regular at 14/min. Pulse 88 bpm, regular. Skin warm and dry. No obvious signs of trauma. Field impression: altered mental status, cause unknown (possible stroke, cardiac event, medication effect, hypoglycemia). Urgent evacuation by helicopter. Activate the satellite communicator for a SAR response. Put him in the recovery position to protect his airway, and monitor him closely while you wait.

Scenario 2: Rock climber with multiple complaints

Setting: A 28-year-old rock climber is rappelling down a cliff when a rope catches, and she falls 8 feet onto a ledge with loose rock. She's conscious and in significant pain: her wrist hurts, her left knee is swelling, and her left shoulder hurts. She's shaken up but insists she's okay. Your group is 3 hours from the trailhead.

Primary survey:

  • X: No massive bleeding visible
  • A: She's alert and talking, so the airway is open (patent)
  • B: She's speaking in full sentences, and her breathing isn't labored
  • C: Radial pulse present, regular, adequate rate. Skin is pink, with normal temperature and moisture.
  • D: Alert and oriented × 3 (knows person, place, time). Pupils equal and reactive.
  • E: An 8-foot fall can cause significant injuries. Expose her to check for hidden ones.

Secondary survey:

  • Head-to-toe exam, focused on where it hurts
  • Left wrist: check for a fracture. NV check beyond the injury. Compare with the right wrist.
  • Left knee: it's swollen. Check NV status. Can she bear weight?
  • Left shoulder: it hurts to move. Check NV and range of motion. Dislocation, fracture, or strain?
  • Vital signs: get a baseline for pain management

Spine check: A fall from height is a spine mechanism, so ask the spine questions before anyone moves. Here she has no neck or back pain, no tingling, and normal hands and feet. Her wrist is a distracting injury, though, so stay conservative. "I'm okay" isn't an exam finding.

Documentation: 28-year-old female, fall 8 feet onto ledge. Alert and oriented. Pain in left wrist (deformity present, NV intact), left knee (swelling, no deformity, able to bear weight), left shoulder (pain with movement, no deformity, NV intact). Vital signs stable. No head injury signs. Field impression: multiple musculoskeletal injuries (left wrist possible fracture; left knee contusion or possible ligament injury; left shoulder strain or possible dislocation). Non-urgent evacuation: she can walk with assistance, or go by litter, depending on how she tolerates it and how hard the terrain is. Splint the wrist and knee. Monitor neurovascular status.

Lesson Summary

The short version

The XABCDE primary survey finds and treats life threats in order of lethality.
Exsanguinating hemorrhage comes before airway. Stop the bleeding first.
The secondary survey is a systematic head-to-toe exam, done after the life threats are stabilized.
SAMPLE for the history and OPQRST for pain guide the rest of the assessment.
Vital signs record where the patient stands. When they change, adjust treatment.
Serial reassessment, every 5-15 minutes depending on stability, shows which way the patient is heading.
A clear SOAP note keeps care continuous when you hand off to higher-level providers.

Assessment drives the plan

What you find sets your treatment priorities and your evacuation decision. Good wilderness medicine is built on a thorough, systematic assessment. Take the time to do it well, and work efficiently. Thorough doesn't mean slow.

Glossary

Primary Survey
The rapid XABCDE assessment that finds and treats life-threatening conditions
Secondary Survey
The full head-to-toe exam you do once the primary survey has stabilized the patient
AVPU
Consciousness scale: Alert, Verbal, Pain, Unresponsive
XABCDE
Primary survey mnemonic: Exsanguinating hemorrhage, Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Expose
OPQRST
Questions that pin down pain: Onset, Provocation, Quality, Radiation, Severity, Time
SAMPLE
History framework: Signs/Symptoms, Allergies, Medications, Past history, Last intake, Events
DCAP-BTLS
Head assessment: Deformity, Contusions, Abrasions, Punctures, Burns, Tenderness, Lacerations, Swelling
Tachycardia
A fast heart rate, typically over 100 bpm at rest. It can mean shock, fear, pain, or fever.
Diaphoretic
Sweating, often cold and clammy. A sign of shock, a cardiac event, pain, or fear.
Field Impression
Your working diagnosis, built from everything the assessment turned up. It guides the treatment plan.
Serial Assessment
Reassessing at set intervals (every 5-15 min) to track how the patient is changing
