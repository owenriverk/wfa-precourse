The landing you didn’t choose.

Free flight’s injury list is short and specific: vertical energy into legs and spine, usually somewhere with no road. This is the ground medicine — what the first person to reach a downed pilot does in the hour before anyone official arrives. Flight safety, weather judgment, and SIV are your instructor’s department.

Hard arrivals

  • Short of the LZ. She came up short, flared into the hillside, and her ankle took the whole arrival. Lower-leg and ankle fractures are the sport’s tax — splint, check the toes, plan the carry.
  • The reserve ride. The toss worked, and he came down under the reserve onto a scree slope, hard on his backside. Now his back hurts. Vertical deceleration is a spine mechanism — treat it like one.
  • The tracker stops moving. A solo XC pilot, a dot that quit updating, and you’re the closest person with boots on. Whoever hikes in is the first responder — what you bring, what you check, how you call it in.

Ground school for the ground

Ordered by how landings actually go wrong:

  1. Spine & Helmets — vertical deceleration is the mechanism this sport manufactures
  2. Musculoskeletal Injuries — ankles and lower legs pay for hard landings
  3. Patient Assessment — a system that works on a 40-degree slope
  4. Airway & Breathing — an unresponsive pilot under a wing needs positioning first
  5. Evacuation — no trail, no road — coordinates, messengers, and the call

Then pressure-test it

Interactive scenarios — one decision at a time, tempting mistakes included, debrief at the end:

  • Don’t Sit Him Up — neck pain, weather inbound, and the urge to prop him up
  • Wiggle, Feel, Warm — the fracture, the splint, and the re-check
  • Two Go, One Stays — getting help to a hillside with no address

The certification question, honestly. “Wilderness First Aid” isn’t a regulated credential. No government body defines what a WFA card means — it means whatever the company that printed it says it means. What counts in front of a patient is whether you can do the work. This course teaches the work, free, and issues a record of completion — not a certificate, and we won’t pretend otherwise. USHPA ratings certify your flying, not your medicine. If a comp, tandem operation, or instructor pathway wants a WFA or WFR card, take that class — this gets you fluent before it and keeps you sharp after. If nobody requires you to hold a card, what you need are the skills, not the laminate.

What this is

Fifteen lessons, a 40-question knowledge check, sixteen practice scenarios, a printable field reference card, and a kit checklist. Free, no account, nothing recorded about you, source on GitHub. It teaches decision-making, not hands-on skill — practice the hands-on parts on a real, padded, complaining friend.
