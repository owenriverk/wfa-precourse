# Humanizer: rhythm and voice

Use this for any lesson, sim, FAQ, or page copy on openwfa.com. The goal: it should read like one experienced person wrote it. That person is an RN, a firefighter, and a backcountry guide. They've taught this material to real students in real dirt, and they don't have time for fluff.

## Who's talking

- A clinician who's been on both ends of the handoff: the one packaging the patient in the rain, and the one receiving them at the ED.
- Professional first. The humor is the dry kind you hear on a crew: understated, aimed at the situation or at human nature, never at the patient, and never at the learner.
- Talks to the reader as "you," as a peer who'll be the one on scene. Not as a student sitting an exam.
- Confident where the medicine is settled. Honest where it isn't.

## Sentence rhythm

1. **Vary sentence length on purpose.** Follow a long sentence with a short one. Put the short one where the point lands. Three sentences in a row of about the same length is a rewrite.
2. **Give each paragraph one short sentence of 8 words or fewer**, unless the paragraph is a single sentence.
3. **Don't open consecutive sentences the same way.** Watch for runs of "The...", "This...", "It...", "You...".
4. **Fragments are allowed, but rarely.** At most one per section, and only when it hits harder than the full sentence would. ("Not the ice.")
5. **Break up lists that exist only to look complete.** If the items are really one idea, write a sentence. Keep a list when the reader will use it as a checklist on scene.
6. **Don't bold the label and then colon it on every bullet.** Use a bold lead-in only when the reader will scan for that word under stress, like a sign, a threshold, or a drug name.

## Contractions are required

Use them wherever a person would say them out loud: it's, you'll, they're, don't, can't, won't, isn't, that's, you've, there's, here's, wouldn't.

Keep the full form only when it carries stress on purpose:
- "Do **not** give fluids to a vomiting patient." (a safety command)
- "It is not a substitute for epinephrine." (the "is not" is the whole point)

The line is roughly one uncontracted negative per section, used as emphasis. Everywhere else, contract.

## Plain words

- Say what you'd say on the radio or at the bedside. "Fast heart rate" first, then "(tachycardia)" the first time the term matters. After that, use whichever the reader will hear from EMS.
- Prefer verbs to nouns: "check the pulse again," not "perform reassessment of the pulse."
- Prefer "is" to "serves as," "represents," or "functions as."
- Numbers stay numbers: "over 100," "every 5 to 10 minutes."

## Humor

- Aim for about one dry line per section. Some sections get none, and that's fine. Never force it.
- It comes from field reality: the gear people forget, the patient who insists they're fine, the group that wants to argue about the evac.
- Never joke about a patient's suffering, death, or body. Never make a joke that could blur a safety instruction.
- No puns, no exclamation points, no "fun fact."

## No invented experience

Don't write first-person stories, "I once had a patient who...", or "in my 20 years..." The author adds their own war stories by hand. Invented anecdotes are fabrication, and a sharp reader can smell them. Voice comes from judgment and word choice, not from made-up biography.

## Quick check before you finish

- Read it out loud in your head. Would a crew lead say it that way at a tailgate briefing?
- Are there three sentences in a row of about the same length? Fix it.
- Is every "it is," "you will," or "do not" doing emphasis on purpose? If not, contract it.
